## PILLAR 5: INSIGHT

### Make Decisions with Purpose

#### Pillar Introduction

Definition. INSIGHT is the team’s capability to make sound, timely, evidence-aware decisions at the point where information is richest. It combines decentralised authority with shared purpose, interconnected measures, regular feedback, clear channels, and transparent information. The INSIGHT led D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Making Suite® consists of Decentralised decision making, Interconnected performance metrics, Regular feedback loops, Empowerment through purpose-based leadership, Clear communication channels, and Transparent information sharing.

Why it matters. In complex work, a decision delayed until the next hierarchy meeting is often a decision made too late. Yet decentralisation without context can create inconsistency and risk. INSIGHT resolves this tension by giving people clear purpose, meaningful boundaries, shared data, and an audit trail. The team does not wait for permission to think; it learns to explain its decisions as: "We are doing X so that Y improves, based on this evidence, within these guardrails."

Research summary. Google's team-effectiveness research highlights structure and clarity, meaning, impact, and psychological safety as important dynamics.2 Together, these dynamics support local decision-making: people need to know what matters, feel safe to raise a concern, understand their decision boundaries, and see the impact of their choices. TEAM IQ gives that research a practical decision system.

Expected outcomes. Teams will use value and risk data to choose work, define pivot thresholds, record significant decisions transparently, and improve their decision process through regular audits.

### Chapter 5.1: Decentralised Decision Making and Purpose-Based Leadership

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to move decision authority closer to the information, communicate purpose rather than over-specify instructions, and use D.I.R.E.C.T Value Routing to select the next best action.

#### Explanation

Centralised decision-making has an understandable appeal: it promises control. But in fast-moving work, leaders at the top often have less current information than the people closest to customers, systems, or delivery constraints. Requiring every decision to travel upward produces delay, filters evidence, and teaches capable people to wait rather than think.

Purpose-based leadership creates a different model. The leader explains the outcome and boundary: "We need to reduce customer waiting time while protecting service quality and cost." The team then has the freedom — and responsibility — to decide how best to do that within agreed limits. The communication is not, "Do X because I said so." It is, "I am proposing X so that Y improves; here is the evidence and the guardrail."

D.I.R.E.C.T Value Routing (DVR) uses the VER, current evidence, and confidence to direct effort toward the highest-value next move. It gives teams a disciplined way to change course when a better option emerges.

#### Practical Example

A customer-support team has three possible automation ideas. Rather than waiting for a quarterly steering committee, the team uses DVR. It sees that an automated password-reset guide has a high VER and low risk, while a broad chatbot replacement has uncertain value and high effort. The team deploys the guide, measures the reduction in contacts, and returns with evidence before considering the larger investment.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — meaning and impact enable judgement. Google notes that meaning can come from work itself or its outcome, and that seeing how work contributes to organisational goals helps reveal impact.2 Purpose-based leadership gives teams this context so local decisions remain aligned with the wider objective.

### Chapter 5.2: Interconnected Metrics and Regular Feedback Loops

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to connect individual, team, and organisational measures, design regular feedback loops, and use Insight Risk Triggers to make timely pivot-or-persevere decisions.

#### Explanation

A team cannot make good local decisions if measures pull people in different directions. For example, a development team rewarded only for speed may create avoidable support cost; a support team rewarded only for call time may transfer customers rather than solve the underlying issue. Interconnected metrics make these trade-offs visible by showing how an individual or functional measure contributes to the shared TVI and to agreed quality guardrails.

Feedback loops keep the measures alive. The team needs a cadence for reviewing what has changed, what has not changed, and what new evidence means for the plan. The Insight Risk Triggers (IRT) provide predefined thresholds: if a risk remains above an agreed exposure by a given date, or if a test fails to produce evidence by a given point, the team must pause, pivot, escalate, or choose a new experiment.

#### Practical Example

A team wants to launch a new data feature. The TVI is customer adoption; the guardrails are accuracy and response time. An IRT is agreed: if accuracy cannot exceed 97% by the end of the second pilot week, the team will not scale the feature. When the threshold is missed, the team pivots to a narrower use case rather than continuing to spend on broad rollout.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — effective teams use multiple perspectives on performance. Google's Project Aristotle combined executive, team-lead, team-member, and quantitative views when studying effectiveness because no single measure captured the full picture.2 Interconnected metrics similarly prevent a team from optimising one local number while damaging the broader outcome.

### Chapter 5.3: Clear Channels and Transparent Information Sharing

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to create clear information channels, record material decisions transparently, and use D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audits to improve decision quality over time.

#### Explanation

Transparent information sharing does not mean broadcasting everything to everyone. It means that the people who need relevant information can find it, understand it, and use it in time. Teams need clear pathways for operational updates, evidence, decisions, risk escalation, and learning. When information is scattered across private conversations, hidden spreadsheets, or meeting memories, the organisation becomes dependent on gatekeepers and vulnerable to misunderstanding.

A D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audit (DDA) creates learning from past choices. It looks at whether the team followed a sound process: Was the purpose clear? Did the team seek evidence? Were assumptions and risks made visible? Was the VER considered when appropriate? Was the decision recorded? The DDA does not punish a decision because the outcome was uncertain. It improves the quality of future decisions.

#### Practical Example

A team decides to delay a release due to a security concern. Three months later, a new manager asks why. The decision log shows the context, risk evidence, TVI guardrails, alternative options, who made the decision, and the planned review. The team can explain the choice quickly and assess whether the conditions have changed, rather than reconstructing the decision from memory.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — safety supports the reporting of concerns. Google notes that in psychologically safe teams, members can bring up problems and tough issues, ask for help, and take interpersonal risks.2 Transparent channels are the operational infrastructure that lets this information become usable rather than remaining private.
