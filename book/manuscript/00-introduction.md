## Why the focus is on teams

The transformation is complete. The framework is in place, the ceremonies are running, the tooling has been bought and the training has been delivered. Three years and a great deal of money later, delivery looks much as it did before: capable people, sensible plans, and a programme that still finishes late and costs more than the case predicted.

Ask why, and the answers are familiar. Priorities moved. A supplier slipped. The dependencies were harder than they looked. None of that is wrong, and none of it explains the thing leaders actually notice: that the same organisation can run one programme brilliantly and another badly, with much the same people and much the same process.

The difference is not in the process. It is in the team. This book is about that difference, why it is about to matter more than it has, and how to measure it and improve it.

### Three pressures that are not going away

**Speed.** The interval between a decision and its consequence keeps shrinking. A competitor ships in weeks what used to take a year. A customer commitment made in a slide deck becomes a fact that the delivery team has to live with. When the market moves faster than the plan, the plan stops being the constraint and the team's ability to decide well, quickly, becomes the constraint.

**Complexity.** Very little of the work now sits inside one team. A release crosses product, engineering, architecture, data, operations, security, legal, a supplier and a regulator. Each holds part of the truth. Nobody holds all of it. Complexity of that kind does not respond to effort; it responds to whether the people holding the parts can combine them into a decision.

**Competition.** Standing out now depends on making something better or different, not on doing the same thing more cheaply. That requires the people closest to the work to generate and test ideas — which is a property of the team, not of the individuals in it.

Add one more pressure, which is the one most organisations are actually feeling: being asked to do more, with less, faster.

### The map is not the terrain

Process work has given what it can. That is not a criticism; it is an observation about what a process is. A process is a description of how work normally flows, captured at a point in time. It is a map. A map is useful, and a map is not the terrain. Walk a route from a map and you will meet the closed road, the washed-out bridge and the path that was never there.

Watch how fast the maps themselves move. The Scaled Agile Framework has been through six major versions since 2011, with many smaller releases in between. Every version is an improvement, and every version also means that whatever was rolled out, trained and embedded across a large organisation is now a version behind. Rollout at that scale commonly takes a year or more. You can finish the training just as the map changes.

This is the trap: treating the framework as the answer, and the team as the thing that implements it. When the framework meets a situation it did not anticipate — which is most days — the framework cannot adapt. The team can. The German field marshal Helmuth von Moltke the Elder put it in a way that has survived: no plan of operations extends with certainty beyond the first encounter with the enemy's main strength. Mike Tyson later reduced it to a sentence everyone remembers.

### Four kinds of problem, one habit of thinking

It helps to know what kind of problem you are in, because different kinds need different thinking.

- **Simple.** Manufacturing, largely. There is a best practice, and following it works. Instruction is appropriate.
- **Complicated.** Most software delivery. There are good practices and no single best one; expertise and analysis decide the route. Instruction alone stops working.
- **Complex.** The situation where nobody has a proven answer and the answer emerges from the people in it. Here you need everyone's context, not the most senior opinion.
- **Chaotic.** Something has broken and the priority is to stabilise.

The habit that causes damage is treating every problem as simple: find the best practice, mandate it, and hold people to the instruction. That works in manufacturing and fails in software, because software is always complex. A team that has only one way of thinking, whatever the situation, will be wrong in three of these four cases.

The alternative is not a better process. It is a team that can read the situation, change how it is thinking, and find a route that nobody has written down yet. Teams that can work without a map, and draw a new one.

### What the research actually found

Two bodies of work sit behind this book. The first is from MIT: studies of **699 people working in groups of two to five**, published in *Science* in 2010. They found a general collective-intelligence factor — a property of the group, not of the individuals in it — that predicts how the group performs across a wide variety of tasks. It is **not strongly correlated with the average or maximum intelligence of the members**. It is correlated with members' social sensitivity, with how evenly conversational time is shared, and with the proportion of women in the group. The finding is uncomfortable for anyone whose answer to a struggling team is to add a cleverer person.

The second is Google's own study, begun in 2011 and running for about four years. It looked for what distinguishes effective teams and landed on five dynamics: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, and impact. The strongest of the five was psychological safety — whether people can say what they actually think without paying a price for it.

Neither study gives you a method. MIT establishes that the property exists and can be measured. Google shows which conditions make it visible. What to do about it, week by week, is the gap this book fills — and the six-pillar model in it is ours, not theirs. The research is the reason to take the subject seriously. The model is the reason you can act on it.

### What we have seen for ourselves

Not every figure in this book comes from a study. In delivery work at a large UK financial services organisation, a programme we were part of saw rework fall by around 47% and the time taken to reach a decision fall by around 60%.

We include it because it is the closest thing we have to a before-and-after from our own practice, and we mark it for what it is: an observation from one organisation, on one programme, in work we were part of. It is not a benchmark, it is not a controlled measurement, and it is not a promise about your team. The organisation is not named, and there is no published result to point you at. Take it as a reason to run the measurement yourself, which the rest of this book shows you how to do.
One thing should be said plainly, because it is usually left out. The collective-intelligence finding is contested. It has been replicated — a 2021 meta-analysis in *PNAS* covering 22 studies, 5,279 people in 1,356 groups found strong support for a general factor, and found that the group's collaboration process mattered more than the average skill of its members — and it has been challenged. A 2017 study in *Intelligence* found that individual IQ accounted for around 80% of the variance in group performance, and a 2024 study in *PLOS ONE* failed to replicate the three predictors.

What is settled is the operational point: some teams reliably outperform what their members' averages would predict. What is still argued about is the precise instrument. Our claim is the narrow one, and it does not depend on that argument being resolved: inspect the conditions, measure them, and see whether the number moves.

### Why artificial intelligence makes this more urgent

Artificial intelligence is the most useful tool to arrive in delivery work for a generation. It is also, in the wrong place, a multiplier of the wrong thing.

An AI tool applied to a broken process does not repair the process. It produces the wrong output faster, in more volume, with more confidence, and it makes the mistake harder to spot because it arrives well presented. Applied to a team that cannot say what it actually knows, it accelerates the thing the team already does: generating material nobody has checked and decisions nobody agreed.

The tool is not the variable. The team is. A team with high collective intelligence will ask the model better questions, judge its output against evidence, notice what looks plausible and is not, and integrate it into a decision. A team without it will generate more, faster, and find out later. The same is true of every previous accelerator: cloud, automation, offshoring, a new framework. Each one increased the consequence of how well the team thought together.

### What this book is

This book is not against process. It is against the assumption that process is the lever.

It sets out six pillars — Target, Expression, Accountability, Mindset, Insight and Quality — a five-stage measurement cycle, and fourteen instruments that turn each pillar into something a team can inspect and change. Every chapter ends with an exercise you can run in one session, something to measure, and one thing to do this week. None of it requires a licence, a subscription or a purchase.

The claim is modest and testable: collective intelligence is a property of a team, it can be measured, and the conditions that produce it can be improved deliberately. If that is true, then the fastest performance gain available to most organisations is not a new framework. It is the team they already have.

### Before you start

One thing, which takes ten minutes and no meeting.

Think of a decision your team got wrong in the last quarter — not a disaster, just a decision that had to be revisited, or that produced work nobody wanted. Write down two things: what information existed somewhere in the team at the time, and who held it. Then ask whether the decision could have been made correctly if everyone had pooled what they already knew.

Almost always, it could. That is the subject of this book.
