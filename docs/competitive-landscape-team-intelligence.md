# Who else does this? The team-intelligence landscape

Researched 2 October 2026. Written to answer one question: **can TEAM IQ Creator call itself
"the first practical framework based on this research"?**

Short answer: **no.** There are at least twenty named candidates, several with validated
instruments and decades of published research behind them, and one published book with the
same title as this offer — *Team Intelligence* — from a major trade publisher in 2025.

What follows is the list, grouped by how close each one sits to the positioning. Where I
verified a competitor directly (6 Team Conditions, QuestWorks, Groops, Team Coaching
International) that is noted; the rest is from their public material and should be re-checked
before it is quoted anywhere.

## E. Free team health checks (the real competition in delivery teams)

Atlassian's **Team Health Monitor** (eight traits, free, embedded in their team playbook),
the **Spotify squad health check**, **Parabol**, **TeamRetro**, and the team self-assessments
inside Scrum.org and SAFe materials. For a delivery team that already runs retros, this is
the incumbent — free, familiar and already in the toolchain. Any paid diagnostic has to beat
"we already do a health check every quarter".

## F. Consultancy frameworks

McKinsey's organisational health work, DDI, RallyBright, and the framework libraries such as
Umbrex all publish team-effectiveness assessment approaches. They compete for the same budget
line, usually at the enterprise end.

## What this means for the claim

"First practical framework based on this research" fails on three counts: the research is
public and shared, the framework space is crowded, and the phrase "Team Intelligence" is
already a book title and a product mark.

**Five claims that are defensible instead.** Each is specific, checkable, and none of them
can be contradicted by a search:

1. **Built for delivery teams, not leadership offsites.** "Six pillars and fourteen instruments
   for teams running complex programmes — not a personality profile and not an away day."
2. **Nothing to license.** "The model is published in the book, the scorecard is free, and the
   instruments are in the chapters. You buy an outside view, not a platform."
3. **A score you can re-run.** "A team-level score across six pillars, re-measured, so the
   question is whether the number moved — not whether the workshop was enjoyed."
4. **Instruments, not just insight.** "Every pillar arrives as an artefact the team uses in the
   work itself: a value card, an evidence log, an outcome contract, a threshold."
5. **We publish where the evidence is contested.** This is the strongest and the rarest —
   see below. Almost nobody in this market discloses the replication debate. Doing so is a
   credibility weapon with a procurement team, and it is true.

## The finding that matters more than the competitor list

While researching this, I found something that affects the site and the book more than any
competitor does. **The collective-intelligence finding is contested in the literature**, and
this offer currently presents it as settled.

What the evidence actually shows:

- **Supports it.** Riedl, Kim, Gupta, Malone and Woolley, *PNAS* 2021: meta-analysis of 22
  studies, 5,279 individuals in 1,356 groups. Strong support for a general collective-intelligence
  factor. The strongest predictor was the group's collaboration process, which mattered more than
  the average skill of members.
- **Challenges it.** Bates and Gupta, *Intelligence* 2017: individual IQ accounted for around 80%
  of the variance in group performance, and the effect involving the proportion of women did not
  replicate.
- **Challenges it further.** Rowe, Hattie and Munro, *PLOS ONE* 2024: failed to replicate
  Woolley's three predictors and argued for a two-factor model instead of a single factor.

The honest position is the one QuestWorks — a competitor — already publishes: *some teams
reliably outperform what their members' averages would predict, that much is well established;
the precise measurement instrument is still being argued about.*

**Recommendation.** Add a short "what this does and does not settle" note to the evidence page,
and a paragraph to the book's research section. It costs nothing commercially: the operating
claim in this offer was never "the c-factor is proven", it is "inspect the conditions and
measure whether they improve". Disclosing the debate makes every other number on the page more
believable, and it turns a weakness in the literature into a differentiator that no competitor
in the list above is using.

Taken from the papers themselves, not from summaries:

| 22 studies, 5,279 individuals, 1,356 groups | Riedl et al., *PNAS* 2021 | Publisher and institutional records |
| Five dynamics; psychological safety the strongest | Google re:Work | The guide |
| Team performance: revenue generated per team member ranged from 1.33 to 15,158.12, logged before analysis | Szatmari, Deichmann & van den Ende, *Strategic Organization* 23(4), 2025 | The paper itself |

These are stronger than the figures we removed, they are all published, and every one of them
can be cited to a source a procurement team can open.

**Correction, made while checking this.** The site previously claimed that team performance
varies roughly 15,000 times more widely than individual performance, citing 2.63-4.89 for
individuals against 1.33-15,158 for teams. That comparison does not hold up. The SAGE paper's
performance measure is revenue generated per team member, in money units, logged before
analysis; its Table 1 gives a minimum of 1.33 and a maximum of 15,158.12. The figures 2.63 and
4.89 appear nowhere in the paper. A money range and a score range cannot be divided to produce
a ratio, and the individual-performance range had no source in the linked paper at all.

The claim has been removed from the homepage, the evidence page, the insight article that
repeated it, and the references. What replaces it is checkable: the team-level dispersion
stated in its own units, and the 43% of group-performance variance from Woolley et al., which
is in that paper.

The lesson generalises. This was the most quantitative claim in the funnel, it sat on the
homepage as a headline metric, and it would have collapsed the moment a procurement analyst
opened the source the page invites them to open.
