## Chapter 20 — Value stream mapping without the ceremony

At the release meeting, the team has a slide for every workstream and a date beside every work item. The product lead wants the reporting capability in front of users before the quarterly review. The design lead wants another pass at the visual animation. Security has asked for evidence that an approval was completed, but nobody is clear who owns the evidence. A developer says the code is ready. Operations has not seen it. The chair asks why a small change has taken so long.

The usual response is to produce a larger plan. People add more columns to the tracker, schedule a mapping workshop and leave with coloured notes that soon become wallpaper. That does not answer the question in the room. The team needs to know what value the work is meant to create, what standard is sufficient for that value, and where time or effort is being consumed before the customer receives anything. Until it can see those things together, it will argue about preference, urgency and blame.

### The principle

Quality starts before a team begins work. It starts by continuing with the end in mind: stating the value to be created, who receives it, how the team will recognise it, and what it will cost to create, support and change. That sequence is deliberately awkward for teams used to receiving a requirement and opening a delivery board. It makes them pause before they turn activity into commitment.

In TEAM IQ, quality is the usefulness and worth of a product, service, process or decision to the customer and the organisation. It is not polish for its own sake, nor a licence to release work that does not meet a necessary standard. A reporting capability that helps people make a decision may be high quality with a plain interface. A beautiful animation can be a poor quality choice when it delays a capability customers need more. The test is value at an appropriate cost and time.

The **7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE)** gives the team a way to make that test explicit. For every quality requirement, it defines a target and a guardrail. The requirements may concern usability, security, reliability, accessibility, performance, accuracy or maintainability. A target says what the work must achieve. A guardrail says what cannot be compromised while achieving it. When the agreed target has been reached, additional effort must compete with the value of other priorities. It may be worth doing, but it is no longer assumed to be good merely because it improves an attribute.

That distinction matters because gold-plating is easy to hide inside professional pride. A specialist can usually name one more refinement. A programme can usually find a reason to postpone a release. Neither observation is an argument against care. It is an argument for making the trade-off visible, whilst there is still a choice. CVE prevents a debate in which one person argues for speed and another argues for quality as if the two are opposites. The real question is whether the next piece of effort creates enough additional stakeholder value to justify what it displaces.

Conducting value stream mapping makes the cost side of that question visible. It follows the work from the moment a need is recognised to the point at which the customer can use the result. Waiting for approvals, unclear handoffs, duplicated data entry, late defect discovery and unnecessary rework are costs even when no invoice is attached to them. They make a small request feel slow, tempt teams to start too much at once, and conceal where a decision is actually stuck.

Google's team-effectiveness research cautions against treating measures such as lines of code or bugs fixed as standalone measures of effectiveness. The same restraint applies here. A busy delivery flow and a long list of completed technical tasks do not establish that the right value reached the customer. CVE and value stream mapping put the outcome, the standard and the flow in the same conversation.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The reporting capability is described as a feature with a fixed deadline. Nobody has written down whose task it improves or what successful use would look like. The design work continues because it is already under way. Security, operations and the product lead each receive a request at different times, with different descriptions of the change. An approval sits in an inbox because the person asked to approve it cannot see the context.

In the review, the team reports that development is complete. Operations reports that it cannot accept the release. Product reports that the capability is late. Each statement is true within one part of the flow, and none explains the whole delay. A defect found near the end is treated as an individual error rather than evidence that work arrived too late for someone to influence it. More meetings are added, but the route through the work remains unseen.

The schedule shows work assigned to people, but not the time it spends in queues. No one can say which handoff is causing the delay, which work is repeated, or which decision is missing. The next status report asks for dates rather than the information needed to remove the constraint. The pattern repeats on the next item.

### What it looks like when it works

Before work begins, the team records the customer task the reporting capability must support and the point at which it will regard the experience as good enough. It records the security and operational conditions that must hold. The design lead can still propose a refinement, but can show what value it adds and what it will postpone. The product lead can make a decision with that information rather than relying on rank or persistence.

The team then draws the actual path of the work. It sees that a single approval is requested only after a build is ready, although the approver needs information available at the start. It moves that conversation forward. It sees that two people copy the same data into separate documents and removes one copy. The map does not make delivery friction disappear. It changes it from a vague complaint into something the people involved can alter. At review, the team can see whether it met its CVE threshold and where it spent time getting there.

The product lead can explain the release choice in terms of the agreed outcome, not personal preference. The team has a visible basis for refusing extra work that does not meet a target or protect a guardrail.

### How to do it

1. **Name the end before naming the work.** For an active item, write a short statement of the value to be created and the person or group who receives it. State the task, decision or service that should be better when the item is complete. Then ask how the team will know. Do not accept a technical output as the answer when the work exists to change a customer's experience or a business result. Also ask what it will cost to create, support and change. The aim is not a perfect forecast. It is to stop those costs being absent from the decision.

2. **Set the 7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE).** Identify the quality requirements that matter for this item. Use the relevant terms directly: usability, security, reliability, accessibility, performance, accuracy and maintainability. For each relevant requirement, agree a target and a guardrail. Make the target observable enough that a review can determine whether it was met. Make the guardrail clear enough that nobody can declare success by sacrificing an obligation which was merely left unstated. Record the standard before development begins, not after a result makes it convenient.

3. **Separate necessary work from optional refinement.** Ask the team to place each proposed activity against the CVE. Does it meet an agreed target? Does it protect a guardrail? Or does it take the work beyond the required level? The third category is not prohibited. It must have a stated value rationale and compete with other priorities. This is where a team prevents gold-plating without asking people to lower their professional standard. It is choosing deliberately where its capacity goes.

4. **Conduct value stream mapping from request to use.** Put the active item at the end of the map and trace backwards through the actual steps that enable it. Include decisions, handoffs, approvals, information received, information re-entered, build work, checking and release. Ask the people who perform each step to describe what happens, rather than drawing the intended process from a policy document. Mark where work waits, returns for clarification, is duplicated or discovers a defect late. Keep the map to the path the item really follows. It does not need decorative symbols or a day-long workshop to be useful.

5. **Find the constraint that changes the whole flow.** Review the map for the earliest practical point at which a missing decision, condition or piece of information could be brought forward. A late operational question may need to be raised when the item is shaped. A repeated data entry may need one shared source. An approval may need a clearer request. Choose one change that removes waiting, repeated work or late discovery. Do not turn the map into a catalogue of every irritation in the organisation. A small change that is adopted is more useful than a large map that is filed away.

6. **Review the decision when the item is ready.** At review, return to the CVE record. Record whether the team stopped at the agreed threshold, exceeded it deliberately with a value rationale, or fell short. If it exceeded the threshold, record what capacity that consumed. If it stopped at the threshold, record what capacity was saved for other priorities. Revisit the value stream map when the work exposed a handoff or wait that had not been visible. This turns quality from a retrospective opinion into a repeatable delivery decision.

### The exercise

Run this exercise with the people responsible for one active work item. Choose an item that has not yet entered development if possible; if it has started, use it to make the next decision clearer. Prepare the current request, any known constraints, and a blank space where everyone can see the CVE and the flow of work. Allow 55 minutes.

- Spend the first 10 minutes agreeing the value the item should create, who receives it, how the team will know, and the expected cost to create, support and change. Capture short statements rather than a new requirements document.
- Spend 15 minutes agreeing the 7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE). List the relevant quality requirements and record a target and guardrail for each. Ask which proposed work is necessary to meet the standard and which goes beyond it.
- Spend 20 minutes tracing the work backwards from customer use to the initial request. Add each actual handoff, approval, repeated entry, wait and checking point. Ask people to describe the work they do, including work that is normally invisible in a plan.
- Use the final 10 minutes to select one source of waiting, duplication or late discovery that the team can change. Record an owner and when the change will be checked.

At the item review, bring back the CVE record. Record whether the team stopped at the agreed threshold, exceeded it deliberately with a value rationale, or fell short. Estimate the capacity saved or consumed by that decision. Keep the map with the item until the selected change has been tested; otherwise it becomes evidence of a meeting, not an aid to delivery.

### What to measure

The artefact is the **7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE)** record for an active work item. It shows the agreed quality targets and guardrails, then the review decision: stopped at the threshold, exceeded deliberately with a value rationale, or fell short. It also records the estimated capacity saved or consumed. Read it when the item is prepared, before development begins, and at the item review. For a team with several active items, read the current records in the regular delivery review to spot repeated choices that are consuming capacity without a stated value rationale. Over time, compare records to see whether the same optional refinement keeps returning and whether it displaces higher-value work or extends delay for customers without a stated rationale.

### One thing to do this week

Choose one item that is about to begin. Before anyone starts development, ask the team to write the customer value, one quality target and one guardrail. Then trace only the next three handoffs it must make before a customer can use it. Do not schedule a mapping event. Put the result beside the item and use it in the next decision.

## Chapter 21 — Customer-centric design

A team has released an improved online form. The delivery board is green. The automated checks have passed, the service is available and the release note has gone out. In the following days, the service desk receives messages from people who cannot tell which option applies to them. Some leave the form unfinished. Others submit it and then contact the organisation to ask what they have agreed to. The team has evidence that the release happened, but very little evidence that the person using it can complete the intended task.

At the next review, the conversation begins with technical activity because that is what the team has ready to show. A test lead explains the checks. A developer explains the changes. The product lead has a list of requests from different customers, none of which can simply be accepted as a priority. What is missing is a shared account of the customer's need, context, behaviour and constraints. Without it, the team will either build the loudest request or defend its existing work. Neither is a reliable way to judge quality.

### The principle

The most expensive quality failure is building the wrong thing perfectly. Customer-centric design begins with the people who receive the work: their need, the setting in which they act, what they do now and the constraints that shape their choices. It asks for enough evidence to understand the problem, test the proposed value and improve the work as customers respond. It does not mean that every request becomes a commitment. It means requests are examined against a real customer problem instead of being sorted by volume or seniority alone.

For a delivery leader, this changes the starting point of design. A feature description is rarely a sufficient account of value. “Add a field”, “make the screen clearer” and “automate the notice” may each conceal several different customer tasks. The team needs to know which task matters, where it currently fails and what a person needs in order to complete it. That inquiry can be brief, but it cannot be replaced by an internal debate about what users probably want.

The **7 C's Outcome Metrics (COM)** moves the primary quality conversation from outputs to outcomes. Output measures continue to have a place. Test coverage, defects, uptime and delivery speed can protect necessary conditions. They are technical guardrails. They do not, by themselves, show whether a customer completed a task, whether retention improved, whether revenue changed or whether the experience was worth its cost. COM connects the team's work to the customer's actual experience and to the business value that follows.

This is a disciplined rather than sentimental approach to the customer. A team may discover that a requested change cannot be justified, that an existing feature should be altered, or that a capability should be retired. Customer-centric design does not promise that the customer will get every proposed answer. It makes the problem, the evidence and the decision visible. That gives product, delivery and operational colleagues a common basis for disagreement.

Continuous customer feedback is what keeps the team from treating a release as the final moment of learning. Before release, the team should have an explicit outcome it expects to see. After release, it should seek feedback and outcome evidence on a regular rhythm. The feedback may show that the technical work is sound but the language is confusing, the steps are in the wrong order, or a constraint was misunderstood. The response is not to abandon technical quality. It is to keep technical evidence as a guardrail and change the primary target to the outcome that matters.

Google's team-effectiveness work combined leader, member, executive and quantitative perspectives because each saw a different part of effectiveness. COM applies the same discipline to quality. Technical, customer and business evidence should be visible together. A green delivery board is useful information. It is not a conclusion about the value of a release.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The online form is designed from an internal policy document and a list of stakeholder comments. The team measures completed development tasks, passed checks and whether the service is up. It treats service-desk messages as an operational issue for another group. When a customer asks for help, the request is added to a backlog with no account of the task that failed.

At review, people defend the measure closest to their own role. The test lead points to coverage. Operations points to uptime. Product points to the length of the backlog. None of these accounts shows whether the person filling in the form can understand it and complete the required task. The team makes a series of small changes based on opinion, then calls the next release an improvement because activity continued. Feedback arrives late, scattered and stripped of the context needed to act on it.

Nobody is accountable for bringing together the customer experience and the business effect. The product lead therefore has to interpret isolated reports after decisions have already been made. The absence of a shared scorecard makes it easy to call a release successful before there is evidence that it solved the original problem for customers in context.

### What it looks like when it works

Before changing the form, the team identifies the customer task and listens to people who carry it out in the relevant context. It records where language, sequence or constraints make the task difficult. It does not accept every suggested feature; it uses the evidence to decide which obstacle is worth addressing first. The team writes the expected customer outcome and the business result alongside the technical conditions that must remain true.

After release, the team makes it easy to collect feedback at the point where customers use the form and brings that feedback into the review. It reads a customer measure, a business measure and a technical guardrail together. If customers are still struggling, the team can see whether the problem is understanding, flow, reliability or something else. It chooses to scale, improve, reposition or retire from evidence, not from attachment to the work already delivered.

Customer feedback is not passed around as anecdote. It is linked to the COM scorecard, so the team can see whether a complaint reflects a repeatable obstacle, a temporary effect or an issue outside the feature. The business representative can also see whether the intended value follows, rather than waiting for a technical report to declare success.

### How to do it

1. **Choose the customer task, not the requested output.** Select the feature, service or decision under discussion and state who receives it. Describe what that person is trying to complete, decide or avoid. Ask for the context in which the task happens and the constraints that affect it. A person using an internal service under time pressure may need something different from a person using the same service occasionally. Keep the statement close to observable behaviour. It should help the team test whether it has improved the task.

2. **Gather evidence before settling on an answer.** Use the customer contact already available to the organisation: direct feedback, service-desk contacts, existing usage evidence or conversations with the people who support the service. Ask what the person was trying to do, what happened, what they did instead and what constraint mattered. Do not ask only whether they like a proposed feature. That question invites a preference. The team needs evidence of the problem and its effect before it decides what to build.

3. **Set the 7 C's Outcome Metrics (COM).** For the proposed release, create a small COM scorecard with at least one customer measure, one business measure and one technical guardrail. The customer measure should show whether people can complete the relevant task or experience the intended benefit. The business measure should show the organisational value that follows, such as retention or revenue where relevant. The technical guardrail should retain a condition the team must protect, such as test coverage, defects, uptime or delivery speed. Choose measures that can influence a decision, rather than measures selected because they are easy to obtain.

4. **Make feedback part of the delivery flow.** Decide before release how feedback will reach the people who can use it. The mechanism must be close enough to the customer experience that it retains context, and regular enough that the team does not wait until a large amount of dissatisfaction has accumulated. Give the team a named point in its review to read feedback alongside COM. The aim is not to collect praise or create an endless survey. It is to find out whether the proposed value is occurring and what needs to change if it is not.

5. **Review at 7, 30 and 60 days.** Use the same COM scorecard at each review. At the earlier review, look for immediate evidence of whether the release can be used as intended and whether a technical guardrail has been affected. At the later reviews, look for the business effect and whether the customer outcome is holding. Record the evidence that supports the next choice. The intervals prevent a release from being declared complete before customers have had a fair chance to respond.

6. **Decide from evidence.** At each COM review, choose one of four actions: scale, improve, reposition or retire the feature. Scaling is justified when the outcome is occurring and the cost remains appropriate. Improving is justified when the customer need remains valid but the current work is not achieving it. Repositioning is justified when the work has value for a different use or group. Retiring is justified when continuing would consume effort without sufficient value. Record the decision and the evidence behind it. This makes it possible to change direction without presenting that change as a failure of the people who built the release.

### The exercise

Use this exercise for one recently released feature. Invite the product lead, a delivery representative, someone who sees customer feedback, and the person responsible for the relevant technical guardrail. Prepare the feature description, any feedback already received and the information needed to read the selected measures. Run a 55-minute session to create the COM scorecard, then schedule the follow-up reviews at 7, 30 and 60 days.

- In the first 10 minutes, state the customer task the feature was meant to improve. Record the customer need, context, behaviour and constraint that the release addresses. Separate this from the team's description of the technical output.
- In the next 15 minutes, identify at least one customer measure and one business measure that would show value, plus one technical guardrail. Capture them as the 7 C's Outcome Metrics (COM) scorecard. Agree where the information will come from and who will bring it to review.
- Spend 15 minutes reviewing available early feedback. Note what customers were trying to do and whether any repeated obstacle is visible. Do not decide a change from a single comment; use the discussion to test whether the scorecard will expose the right evidence.
- In the final 15 minutes, book the 7, 30 and 60 day reviews and agree that each will decide whether to scale, improve, reposition or retire the feature.

At each later review, read the same scorecard rather than replacing it with a new list of delivery measures. Record the decision, the evidence behind it and any change needed to the feature or feedback mechanism. The session establishes the loop; the later reviews close it.

### What to measure

The artefact is the **7 C's Outcome Metrics (COM)** scorecard. For each recently released feature, it contains at least one customer measure, one business measure and one technical guardrail. Its purpose is to show customer and business impact without discarding technical evidence. Read the scorecard at 7, 30 and 60 days after release, alongside customer feedback, and record whether the feature should scale, improve, reposition or retire. Keep the technical guardrail visible, but do not allow it to stand in for the customer outcome. Make it a standing item in the feature review until the 60-day decision has been recorded, even if technical delivery has moved on. This prevents the customer outcome being lost when the next release takes attention.

### One thing to do this week

Take one recent release to the next team review. Ask the people present to name the customer task it was meant to improve. Add one customer measure and one business measure beside the technical guardrail you already read. Book a 30-day review now, with someone who can bring customer feedback. That is enough to begin treating the release as a test of value rather than a completed output.

## Chapter 22 — Quality, cost and the cross-functional team

The team is ready to approve an automated customer-notification process. The business case says it is a short build. Development has selected an approach. Near the end of the meeting, someone from customer support asks who will handle messages that do not arrive. Operations asks how delivery failures will be monitored. A colleague responsible for customer communications asks about message templates and opt-out rules. Security asks how contact data will be protected. The room becomes quiet because none of these questions belongs neatly inside the original build estimate.

The wrong response is to treat the questions as delay caused by people outside the team. Each one describes work or risk that will exist after the build is complete. If the questions are left until release, the team may find a defect, a compliance concern or an operating cost when change is difficult and expensive. If they are heard early, the team can compare its initial approach with an alternative that may cost more to build but less to maintain, support, secure or retire. Quality requires that wider view.

### The principle

Corrective quality assurance is not merely testing near the end in the hope of finding defects before customers do. It is the practice of asking quality questions while decisions are still cheap to change. Security, accessibility, operational support, customer communications, data protection, performance, legal constraints and maintainability are not add-ons that can be attached when a build appears complete. They are conditions that can alter the design, the cost and the value of the work.

This is preventative in effect, even though the TEAM IQ practice is called Corrective QA. It corrects the course early by bringing evidence into the decision. A team that waits for a final checking stage often turns quality into a contest between the people trying to release and the people asked to approve. The later the question is raised, the more likely it is to be framed as an obstacle. Asked when the work is still being shaped, the same question can inform the design and prevent avoidable rework.

Lifecycle cost analysis completes the picture. Build cost is visible because it has a plan, people and a date. The cost of maintaining message templates, handling opt-out rules, monitoring delivery failures, responding to customer complaints, securing data and retiring old channels may be spread across several groups and future budgets. That does not make it optional. A feature that is inexpensive to create may be expensive to support, maintain, secure or retire. Conversely, an approach that looks costly at the outset may remove recurring cost or customer harm.

The purpose is not to select the lowest build cost, or to insist on the most elaborate answer. It is to make the value-versus-cost trade-off visible over the full life of the work. That requires more than the view of a single discipline. Development understands build cost. Operations sees reliability. Support sees customer pain. Finance sees economic effect. Security sees risk. Product sees desired value. Customer communications sees what a message means to the person receiving it. Each account is incomplete by itself.

Cross-functional collaboration brings those accounts into the decision early, around a shared outcome rather than a late approval gate. Woolley and colleagues' findings on social sensitivity and balanced turn-taking indicate that groups benefit when different perspectives can be expressed and integrated. In delivery terms, diversity of function is only useful when the information changes a decision. Inviting colleagues to a meeting after the scope has been fixed is not collaboration. Giving their evidence a place before approval to build is.

The **Lifecycle Value Review** is the point at which the team does this visibly. It looks across quality, cost and risk before a major commitment. It makes the assumptions in a business case open to challenge and gives the team a record of issues identified before development. The result may be a different approach, a clearer condition for proceeding or a decision not to pursue the work. Those are quality decisions, not failures of momentum.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The notification process is handed from product to development with a short build estimate. Development completes the work against the stated requirement. Operations sees it during release preparation, when monitoring is hard to add without changing the design. Support receives a briefing after customer messages begin to fail. Customer communications finds that templates do not cover the situations customers encounter. Security is asked to sign off on contact data once the route through the system has already been chosen.

The team calls the resulting work unexpected. Defects and rework are entered as delivery problems, whilst the original decision remains unquestioned. Finance sees a build cost that looked low; other groups absorb maintenance, support and customer costs elsewhere. People protect their own deadlines by handing the work on quickly. No one owns the whole lifecycle, so no one can explain whether the original value still justifies its cost.

Because the issue is found late, the team now has a choice between accepting it, delaying the release or working around it. None of those choices is as good as a design decision made when the relevant people first understood the proposed value and its lifecycle demands for every group involved in delivery from the outset.

### What it looks like when it works

Before approval to build, the team brings development, operations, support, finance, security, product and the relevant customer communications perspective into the same Lifecycle Value Review. They start with the intended value and trace what the work will require to create, run, support, secure, change and retire. The questions are specific: how failures will be noticed, how customers will be helped, how opt-out rules will be handled, and what happens to legacy channels.

The initial approach remains possible, but it is no longer the only option in the room. The team may choose an existing communications platform because it reduces lifecycle cost and compliance risk, even if the build takes slightly longer. It records the material quality, cost and risk issues identified before development, together with the assumptions made. At the end of the first release, it compares those assumptions with early operational evidence and updates the business case. Work has not crossed a wall. The disciplines have made one decision with different evidence.

The review gives support and operations time to prepare for the chosen route, while development can adjust before change becomes rework. The team can explain the trade-off plainly to sponsors, including what the chosen approach will cost after release.

### How to do it

1. **Start with the value and the full life of the work.** Before approval to build, state the customer and organisational value the initiative is meant to create. Then name what will happen after the initial build: it may need to be operated, supported, monitored, secured, changed and eventually retired. Do not begin by asking each function whether it approves a completed proposal. Begin by asking what the proposed value will demand from each part of the lifecycle. This keeps the discussion attached to the outcome rather than to departmental preference.

2. **Bring Corrective QA into the shaping decision.** Identify the quality questions that could alter the design while change remains possible. Consider security, accessibility, operational support, customer communications, data protection, performance, legal constraints and maintainability. Invite the people with direct knowledge of those conditions to explain what they see, including what could fail and what evidence would show it. Corrective QA is useful when it creates a design choice or an explicit condition for proceeding. It is less useful when it becomes a late list of objections with no time left to respond.

3. **Analyse cost through the lifecycle.** Set the build estimate beside the work required to maintain, support, secure and retire the initiative. For the notification process, that includes message templates, opt-out rules, monitoring delivery failures, customer complaints and legacy channels. For another initiative, the details will differ, but the question remains: where will recurring effort, customer harm or risk arise after release? Do not pretend that every implication can be priced precisely before a decision. Record the implication and its owner so that the team can compare approaches without allowing unseen work to disappear from the case.

4. **Run a Lifecycle Value Review before approval to build.** Use the review to examine quality, cost and risk together. Ask each function what the initiative needs from it during the lifecycle and what assumption could prove false. Look for an approach that protects the intended value at an appropriate cost, not simply the approach with the quickest initial build. Capture material issues, the proposed response, the assumption and who will revisit it. The review should end with a clear decision: proceed with the chosen approach, change the approach, or defer until a material issue can be resolved.

5. **Make cross-functional collaboration continuous.** A single review does not mean the lifecycle view is complete. Keep the people who hold operational, support, security, financial, product and customer communications evidence connected to the initiative at the points where their information can change a decision. Share the intended outcome and the recorded assumptions, so that a concern is assessed against the same purpose rather than treated as a private escalation. When a new condition appears, ask whether it changes value, cost, quality or risk across the whole lifecycle.

6. **Compare prediction with early evidence.** At the end of the first release, return to the Lifecycle Value Review. Compare predicted lifecycle implications with early operational evidence: what support was needed, what monitoring revealed, what customer contacts showed, and what conditions required further work. Update the business case. This is not a search for blame when an assumption proves wrong. It is the step that makes future cost analysis more credible and stops a decision from remaining frozen at the point of approval.

### The exercise

Run this exercise before approval to build for the next major initiative. Invite the people who can speak to development, operations, support, finance, security, product and the relevant customer communications or data protection considerations. Prepare the initial business case, the intended value and the proposed approach. Give the group 60 minutes for a Lifecycle Value Review.

- Spend the first 10 minutes stating the value the initiative is meant to create and the customer or organisational outcome it should support. Read the initial build assumption without treating it as fixed.
- Use the next 20 minutes to identify quality questions early: security, accessibility, operational support, customer communications, data protection, performance, legal constraints and maintainability where they apply. Capture material quality, cost or risk issues that could change the approach.
- Spend 20 minutes tracing the lifecycle cost implications: creating, maintaining, supporting, securing, changing and retiring the work. Ask what each function would need to do, what could recur, and which assumption needs evidence after release.
- Use the final 10 minutes to decide whether the initiative can proceed, needs a changed approach or must wait for an issue to be resolved. Record the number of material quality, cost or risk issues identified before development and the person responsible for each follow-up.

At the end of the first release, reconvene briefly. Compare the predicted lifecycle implications with early operational evidence and update the business case. Retain the review record with the initiative; it shows whether cross-functional evidence changed the work while that change was still possible.

### What to measure

The artefact is the **Lifecycle Value Review**. Before approval to build, it records the number of material quality, cost or risk issues identified, the lifecycle implications considered, the assumptions made and the agreed response. Read it at the approval decision and again at the end of the first release. At that point, compare predicted lifecycle implications with early operational evidence and update the business case. A growing list of late issues is not a target to celebrate; it is a prompt to bring the relevant perspective earlier next time. Read it with the same cross-functional group where a changed assumption affects the intended outcome or could alter the chosen approach before that effect is handed downstream and harder to correct later.

### One thing to do this week

For the next initiative waiting for approval, invite one colleague outside development who will live with the result after release. Ask them one question: what will this require to operate, support, secure or retire? Record the answer beside the build estimate and bring it to the approval discussion.

You now have six pillars, a cycle and a set of instruments. They give you a way to examine how teams work, choose a change, observe its effect and decide what to do next. In this final pillar, quality keeps that cycle connected to the value a customer receives, the cost the organisation carries and the people whose work makes both visible.
