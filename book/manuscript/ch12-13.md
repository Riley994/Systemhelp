## Chapter 12 — Roles, and modelling accountability

At the Friday delivery review, the programme plan was green until someone asked whether the new service could move into operational support. The delivery manager believed the service desk had accepted it. The service desk lead believed the product team was still finishing the knowledge articles. The product manager had asked an analyst to draft them, but the analyst thought the technical writer would publish them. None of those people had acted carelessly. Each had completed a sensible piece of work. The handover still failed because nobody could point to the outcome, the person leading it, the evidence required, or the moment it would be reviewed.

The meeting then did what such meetings often do. It searched for a name. People opened messages, recalled conversations and tried to establish who had promised what. That is slow, awkward and usually fruitless. A name in an email is not the same thing as a role that leads an outcome. The preceding work on clarity should have made the destination and expectations visible. Accountability makes that clarity usable when work changes hands and pressure rises.

### The principle

**Accountability** is the team’s shared certainty about who owns which outcomes, what evidence will demonstrate progress, how handoffs work, and how people will respond when conditions change. Blame produces defensive reporting and carefully narrow commitments; accountability makes work visible early enough for the team to act.

There are two levels. Individual accountability means knowing what you have committed to and following through. Team accountability means behaving like total football: covering, supporting, escalating and picking up when the outcome requires it. A person can fulfil an assigned task whilst the team still misses the outcome. The team can support a colleague without dissolving the clarity of who is leading the work.

A role is the contribution an outcome needs; a name is the person currently filling it. Confuse the two and your plan depends on memory. A named individual may move team, take leave, or be drawn into an urgent incident. The outcome still needs a lead, contributors, people to consult and people who need to know. The team also needs a shared answer to what complete means. That is role clarity: a working arrangement that can be inspected, not a job title copied from an organisation chart.

The cost of its absence is familiar. Two people prepare the same material. A risk lives between workstreams because each person has assumed it sits elsewhere. A task is closed because it was sent, although the receiving team cannot use it. Near the end of a release, an issue appears that was visible all along but owned by nobody as an outcome. More meetings will not repair this if the meetings merely circulate activity.

The D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC) is the practical countermeasure. It is a short, visible commitment that states the expected outcome, the metric it should influence, the evidence that will prove the result and when that evidence will be reviewed. It changes the question from whether someone completed a task to whether the work created the expected change, and what has been learned when it did not. A task may be necessary. It is not, on its own, the promise the team needs.

Google’s Project Aristotle identified dependability and structure and clarity as important team dynamics. In that work, dependability includes completing quality work on time, while structure and clarity include understanding job expectations, how to fulfil them and the consequences of performance. A DOC turns those broad expectations into evidence that a team can read together.

The leader is part of the mechanism, not an observer of it. When a leader protects their own missed commitment with a vague explanation, the team learns to conceal uncertainty. When the leader shows the evidence, explains the changed condition or flawed assumption, states the learning and agrees the next action, the team has a standard worth copying. That example is especially powerful because it happens when the stakes are real. The next chapter takes this standard to the boundaries between people and the regular conversations that keep agreements alive.

### What it looks like when it is missing

A portfolio has an objective to make a customer service available to operations. The delivery board lists several activities: finish testing, write support material, train the service desk and obtain approval. Each activity has a person’s name beside it. No one has stated which role leads the operational outcome or what evidence proves that the service is usable by the receiving team.

Testing finishes and the tester reports completion. The technical writer sends a draft. The service desk manager receives an invitation to training but cannot see the material. At the review, the programme lead asks for a status. Each person gives an accurate account of their own effort. The board hears four updates and still cannot establish whether operations can take the service.

A late defect then exposes an assumption about access rights. The discussion begins with who should have noticed it. People protect their area, because an admission may be treated as a personal failure. The issue becomes a sequence of explanations. No one records the assumption, the evidence that disproved it, or the action needed to recover. The operational outcome remains unowned. The plan stays green until it is suddenly not. The same gap can return in the next release under a different label.

### What it looks like when it works

The same service has one visible outcome: operations can support it under agreed conditions. The team identifies the lead role, the contributors, the people who must be consulted and those who need to know. The person currently filling the lead role is named, but the board makes clear what the role must achieve if that person changes.

Its D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC) links the outcome to a relevant measure and names the production evidence the team will inspect. The review date is on the board before work starts. Testing, support material, access and training remain pieces of work, but they are connected to the outcome rather than reported as separate destinations.

When the access assumption proves wrong, the lead brings the evidence to the review. They say what changed, what the team has learned and what needs to happen next. The delivery lead does not turn this into a performance trial. The team gets help with the dependency, adjusts the next action and preserves the evidence with the DOC. Everyone can see the effect on the intended service. The review can focus on recovery, not reconstruction. The outcome may still be delayed, but the delay is explicit and the learning remains available.

### How to do it

Start with critical outcomes, not the complete task list. Select commitments that affect a target, a key quality measure, a material risk or a handoff that could stop delivery. Make the outcome observable. “Prepare the support pack” describes effort. “Operations can support the service using agreed material and access” describes a condition the team can inspect.

1. **Identify the role required by the outcome.** Ask who is leading the outcome, who is contributing, who needs to be consulted and who needs to know. Then name the person filling each relevant role for this cycle. Keep the role and the name separate on the working board.

2. **Write a D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC).** Keep it short enough to read during normal delivery work. Record the expected outcome, the metric it should influence, the evidence that will prove the result and the review point. If the entry only says that a document will be written or a meeting held, it is not yet an outcome contract.

3. **Test the contract against the actual work.** Ask what counts as complete, what assumptions must hold and what dependency could prevent the result. If another team supplies an input, make the condition visible now. Do not make a person promise an outcome that depends on a decision they cannot obtain; make the dependency and required support visible instead.

4. **Make individual and team accountability explicit.** The individual filling the lead role owns the commitment to bring evidence and learning. The team owns the conditions around it: decisions, dependencies, support and escalation. This prevents the false choice between leaving someone alone with a difficult problem and having everyone vaguely responsible. One person leads; the group acts when the outcome requires it.

5. **Review evidence, not confidence.** At the stated review point, look at the metric and the agreed evidence before accepting a status. A contract can be achieved, partially achieved, disproved or blocked. Those classifications describe what happened without pretending every outcome was under complete control. A blocked result should show the condition that blocked it and the action needed from the team or outside it.

6. **Model the response you expect.** Leaders should put their own material commitments through the same discipline. If a commitment is missed, show the evidence. Explain the changed condition or the flawed assumption without hiding behind it. State the learning, agree the next action and record it where the team can find it. Do this before asking others for their status. It is a practical signal that bad news is information, while unexplained status is not acceptable.

7. **Keep the contract alive when conditions change.** A DOC is not a promise to ignore reality. A decision, supplier delay or new evidence can change the route. Update the contract openly, including the reason and the next review. Do not quietly replace an uncomfortable outcome with an easier task. The point is to retain the link between the intended change, the evidence and the learning.

This discipline also keeps the preceding clarity work honest. Expectations that cannot be expressed as an outcome, a measure and evidence are still too loose for delivery. Once the team can see its commitments this way, it is ready to manage the boundary conditions around them rather than hoping that a name beside a task will be enough.

### The exercise

Run a 55-minute working session before the next work cycle. Prepare a list of the major commitments already planned, the current targets and key quality measures, and any known handoffs. Bring the delivery board or a shared document that the team will continue to use. Ask the people who lead the relevant work to attend; this is not a status meeting for people who cannot shape the commitments.

Spend the first 10 minutes listing the major commitments in plain language. Remove activity labels that cannot be tested as an outcome. For each remaining item, take 15 minutes to identify the lead role, contributors, people to consult and people who need to know. Record the person currently filling each role, but preserve the role description beside the name.

Use the next 20 minutes to create a D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC) for each priority commitment. For every DOC, capture the expected outcome, the metric it should influence, the evidence the team will inspect and the review point. Ask the group to challenge any contract that reports a task rather than a change. Where an assumption or dependency makes the result uncertain, record it rather than forcing a confident status.

Use the final 10 minutes for a leader-led review of one commitment. The leader should practise the response required when evidence is weaker than expected: show the evidence, explain the condition or assumption, state the learning and agree the next action. Capture the completed DOCs in the normal delivery board, together with the review dates. The output is not a separate workshop pack. It is the set of visible commitments the team will read during the cycle.

### What to measure

The artefact is the **D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC)**. Review all major commitments in the next work cycle and calculate the percentage expressed as complete DOCs. At each DOC review, classify the result as achieved, partially achieved, disproved or blocked. For every result other than achieved, retain the evidence and learning; never accept an unexplained status label.

Read the DOCs at their stated review points and inspect the percentage at the cycle review. The measure reveals whether critical work has an outcome contract, not whether people have been kept busy. It also makes missing evidence visible before the cycle ends. This is an early warning for delivery leaders. A falling percentage points to commitments that are still being treated as tasks.

### One thing to do this week

Choose one major commitment due in the next work cycle. With the person leading it, write a D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC) on the delivery board: expected outcome, metric, evidence and review point. At the next team meeting, ask what condition could disprove it and what the team would need to do if that happened. Do not wait for a wider process change.

## Chapter 13 — Check-ins, empowerment and agreements

On Thursday afternoon, a release manager learns that the integration test has failed against a changed data field. The software team says the change was sent to data engineering on Monday. Data engineering says the message asked for a review, not for a usable schema. The architect assumed the interface was still being explored. A customer demonstration is scheduled for the next morning. Everyone can produce a message that supports their own reading of the situation, but no message says what the receiver needed, when they needed it, or how either side would know the handoff was ready.

The immediate temptation is to make the release manager approve every future exchange. That adds a queue and leaves the real defect untouched. The problem is at the edge between contributions: boundaries were implied rather than agreed. The role work in the previous chapter establishes who leads an outcome. The next task is to make the conditions around that work visible, inspect them regularly and give people authority to act within limits they can see.

### The principle

Accountability often fails between roles rather than inside them. A developer can reasonably believe that design is approved whilst an architect believes the developer is still exploring. A product manager can believe that customer validation is under way whilst the people closest to customers have not received the material. Such gaps are rarely laziness. They are the predictable result of a boundary that exists in somebody’s head but not in the team’s working agreement.

A **Quantified D.R.E.A.M Boundaries (QDB)** board maps people and roles to the outcomes that matter. The team places each target, key quality measure or material risk on a visible board. It assigns a single primary owner, named contributors and measurable handoff conditions. It is not an expanded organisation chart. It is a current picture of the moments where one part of delivery needs another part to act.

The word *handoff* needs more discipline than it usually receives. Sending an item is an action by the sender. A complete handoff is a condition for the receiving role: it can use the item because the agreed information, quality and timing conditions are met. A link to a draft, a meeting invitation or a message saying “over to you” may be useful, but none proves that condition. The receiver must be able to tell quickly whether they have what they need to proceed.

Regular check-ins make the board a working agreement rather than a diagram produced once and forgotten. They provide a short, repeatable place to inspect upcoming boundaries, evidence from recent ones, decisions needed and changed conditions. They should not become a tour of individual activity. Their value lies in exposing a boundary before it becomes an urgent defect, and in agreeing the next action while the people who can act are present.

Authority close to the work follows clarity; it cannot safely precede it. A person needs to know the purpose, the boundary and the measures that matter before making a local decision. They also need to know which decisions are theirs, which must be consulted on and which require escalation. Without those conditions, a leader may call for initiative but still reserve every meaningful decision. That creates delay and teaches the team to wait.

Google’s Project Aristotle found structure and clarity to be an important team dynamic, including understanding job expectations, the process for fulfilling them and the consequences of performance. QDB makes those expectations specific to a target, quality measure or risk, and makes the conditions of a handoff inspectable. The leader’s task is not to remove every limit. It is to set clear limits, hold the agreed review rhythm and respond to evidence rather than preference.

These agreements are a bridge to the mindset work that follows. A team cannot take distributed responsibility merely because a leader asks it to. It needs practice in keeping small, observable agreements, revising them openly when evidence changes, and using a failed handoff to improve the boundary instead of blaming the sender.

### What it looks like when it is missing

A release has a weekly meeting, but the agenda is a round of status updates. The software lead reports that a schema change is complete. Data engineering reports that data quality is being monitored. The architect reports that the interface design is progressing. These statements can all be true. No one asks whether the receiving team has the information, quality and timing conditions needed to use the change.

The board has a broad entry for data quality, with several names beside it. During the week, each group assumes another group will spot an incompatible field. An issue is found only when an integration test fails. The sender argues that the file was supplied. The receiver argues that it lacked the needed definition. The work is returned without a clear condition for the next attempt.

Decision-making then moves upwards. People send questions to the release manager because they cannot see what they may decide locally. The manager becomes the route for small choices and the check-in becomes a queue. When a handoff fails, the conversation concentrates on who delayed it. Missing information, a quality issue, a timing issue, an unclear owner and an untested assumption are all collapsed into the same accusation.

### What it looks like when it works

The same release has a Quantified D.R.E.A.M Boundaries (QDB) board that shows the material target, quality measure and risk. The relevant boundary names one primary owner and the contributors, then states what a usable schema change requires. The data engineering lead owns the data-quality TVI. The software lead owns schema-change notice. The handoff is complete only when an automated contract test passes and the data dictionary is updated.

At a short check-in, the people involved read the condition rather than reconstructing intent from messages. The automated test has not passed, so the handoff is not presented as complete. The software lead can correct the change within the agreed boundary. The data engineering lead can assess the data-quality TVI. A decision that exceeds those limits is identified quickly and escalated with the evidence needed.

When the handoff later fails first time because an assumption was untested, the team records that cause. It adjusts the QDB condition and checks it at the next review. Nobody needs to pretend that sending the file was enough, and nobody waits for a manager to approve a correction already within their authority. The next check-in confirms the correction before the demonstration. The agreement is visible, testable and revised by learning.

### How to do it

Build the QDB around material edges in the next two work cycles, not around every conversation. Start where delivery has repeatedly slowed, where a quality problem has appeared late, where a decision waits too long, or where a risk crosses teams. Gather the targets, key quality measures and material risks already in use. The task is to make the connections between them usable in the rhythm of work.

1. **Put each material target, key quality measure and material risk on the QDB board.** Use language the delivery teams already recognise. A board full of broad labels cannot guide a decision. Make each item specific enough that people can see when it is affected and who needs to talk.

2. **Set the boundary for each item.** Assign a single primary owner and name the contributors. This is not a second role catalogue. It answers a narrower question: who brings the boundary and its evidence to the check-in, and who must contribute for the condition to be met? Where no one can take the primary owner position, the boundary is not ready to manage.

3. **Write measurable handoff conditions.** For each handoff, specify the information, quality and timing conditions the receiver needs. Make the wording receiver-centred: the receiving role can use the output because the agreed conditions are met. State the evidence that demonstrates each condition. An automated test, an updated data dictionary, a signed decision or a reviewed item can be evidence where they are relevant. A vague assurance is not.

4. **Make decision authority visible.** For the primary owner and contributors, state the purpose of the boundary, the measure that matters and the decisions they may make to keep work moving. Also state what requires consultation or escalation. This is how authority moves nearer to the work without creating conflicting changes. The boundary gives people a safe operating limit; it does not require them to guess a leader’s preference.

5. **Hold brief, regular check-ins at the working cadence.** Read the QDB board before the meeting so the conversation can focus on changes. Inspect boundaries that are due, evidence from those just completed, decision points and risks to the next handoff. Ask whether the receiver can use the output now. If not, identify which agreed condition is absent and who will act. End with recorded actions and any decision needing escalation.

6. **Treat agreements as observable commitments.** Keep the QDB where both sender and receiver can see it. When a condition changes, change it openly, name the reason and agree the new timing. Do not allow a private message to replace the shared agreement. This protects the team when people are absent and prevents an informal exception becoming the assumed process.

7. **Learn from failed handoffs.** For the next two work cycles, classify each failed material handoff as missing information, quality issue, timing issue, unclear owner or untested assumption. Discuss the classification at the check-in, then improve the QDB condition rather than blame the sender. A failed condition may show a gap in the agreement, not a lack of effort.

The regular rhythm matters because delivery conditions move. A QDB that is precise on Monday may need revision after a new risk, a changed decision or evidence from a test. The aim is neither a large governance meeting nor unrestricted local action. It is a visible agreement that lets capable people act quickly within clear limits and raise the right issue before it becomes a release surprise.

### The exercise

Run a 60-minute QDB working session for the next two work cycles. Prepare the current delivery targets, key quality measures, material risks and a list of the handoffs most likely to affect them. Bring evidence from any recent late defect, returned item or delayed decision. Include people from both sides of each selected handoff; a boundary cannot be agreed by the sender alone.

Use the first 10 minutes to select no more than three material boundaries. Put each associated target, key quality measure or risk on the shared board. In the next 15 minutes, assign the single primary owner and named contributors for each boundary. Confirm that the primary owner can bring evidence to the regular check-in and can obtain the required contribution.

Spend 20 minutes writing the measurable handoff conditions. For each one, record the information, quality and timing the receiving role requires, plus the evidence that proves the condition. Ask the receiver to read the condition back in their own words. If they cannot tell whether they can use the output, make the condition more precise.

Use the next 10 minutes to agree decision authority inside each boundary: what the people closest to the work may decide, what needs consultation and what needs escalation. Use the final five minutes to set the check-in cadence and capture the agreed QDB board in the team’s normal workspace. At the first check-in, review one completed or failed handoff using the agreed conditions. Keep the record as a living working agreement, not a workshop output.

### What to measure

The artefact is **Quantified D.R.E.A.M Boundaries (QDB)**. For the next two work cycles, track the percentage of material handoffs that meet their agreed QDB condition first time. Read the measure at the regular check-in and use the cycle review to identify patterns.

Record both the total material handoffs and those meeting the condition first time, so the percentage can be checked rather than assumed at each cycle review. When a handoff fails, classify the cause as missing information, quality issue, timing issue, unclear owner or untested assumption. Keep the classification with the boundary evidence. The purpose is to improve the condition, not to create a league table of senders. Repeated failure in one class tells the team where the agreement needs attention.

### One thing to do this week

Pick one handoff due before the next delivery check-in. With the sender and receiver, write its information, quality and timing conditions on the Quantified D.R.E.A.M Boundaries (QDB) board. Agree what evidence will show that the receiver can use it, and what decision the people involved can make without waiting for a manager. Read that condition at the check-in. Keep it visible to both parties.
