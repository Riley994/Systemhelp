# Verified figures, and where each one comes from

Every number used on the website or in the book should be traceable to a line in this file.
Where a figure has a caveat, the caveat is recorded here too, and should appear wherever the
figure is used. Compiled 2 October 2026 from the publications themselves where they were
available, not from summaries.

---

## 1. Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi & Malone (2010)

"Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups",
*Science* **330**(6004), 686–688. DOI 10.1126/science.1193147.
https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1193147

**Design.** Two studies. Study 1 ran 40 groups; Study 2 ran 152 groups — 192 groups in total.
**699 people**, working in groups of two to five. The figure of 700 in the evidence register is
a rounding, not an error.

**Timeline.** The paper was received 5 April 2010, accepted 13 September 2010, and published
29 October 2010. It states no study duration. The research programme predates submission, so
describing it as several years of work is a reasonable inference from the timeline rather than
a citation error. Do not assert a specific number of years as though the paper had given one.

**The finding.** A general collective-intelligence factor ("c") that explains a group's
performance across a wide variety of tasks. One factor with an initial eigenvalue accounting
for **more than 43% of the variance**.

**What c does and does not correlate with.**

| Measure | Correlation with c | Significance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average member intelligence | r = 0.15 | P = 0.04 (weak but significant) |
| Maximum member intelligence | r = 0.19 | P = 0.008 (weak but significant) |
| Average social sensitivity ("Reading the Mind in the Eyes") | r = 0.26 | P = 0.002 |
| Variance in speaking turns | r = –0.41 | P = 0.01 |
| c predicting the criterion task | r = 0.52 | P = 0.01 |

In the regression, average member intelligence (b = 0.05, ns) and maximum member intelligence
(b = 0.12, ns) were **not** significant once c was included. The paper's own conclusion is that
c "was still a much better predictor of group performance on the criterion tasks than the
average or maximum individual intelligence".

**Wording discipline.** The paper says c is **"not strongly correlated"** with individual
intelligence. Two stronger versions are both wrong: a correlation of zero, and "no correlation
at all" — the raw correlations were 0.15 and 0.19 and were significant. "No significant
correlation" is defensible as a statement about *prediction* (neither was a significant
predictor in the model), which is how the site uses it. The figures and P values are given in
the source list so the claim can be checked.

**Also found.** Group cohesion, motivation and satisfaction did **not** predict group
performance — a useful point, and one that cuts against the softer end of the market.

## 2. Google — re:Work, Project Aristotle

"Understand team effectiveness", re:Work guide.
https://rework.withgoogle.com/guides/understanding-team-effectiveness

**Five dynamics** distinguish effective teams: psychological safety, dependability, structure
and clarity, meaning, and impact. Psychological safety was the most important. Research ran
from 2011 for about four years.

**Design.** Project Aristotle studied **180 teams** — 115 project teams in engineering and 65
pods in sales — ranging from **three to fifty individuals**, deliberately mixing high and low
performers, and measured effectiveness four ways. The guide states no duration; the site's
"four years" is a reasonable inference from a 2011 start to the 2015–16 publication of the
findings, not a claim the guide makes.

**The caveat worth quoting** — Google's own guide says that the variables which were not
significant in their study may matter elsewhere: *"while these variables did not significantly
impact team effectiveness measurements at Google, that doesn't mean they're not important
elsewhere."*

This matters strategically. Google's study ran inside a company that had already filtered
heavily for individual ability, so the range of individual intelligence in the sample was
restricted — which attenuates any correlation it could have detected. Google themselves
decline to generalise the null results. Their study therefore supports "given a uniformly
high-competence population, the remaining variance is in how the team works" and does **not**
support "team fit matters more than competence".

## 3. Szatmari, Deichmann & van den Ende (2025)

"The increasing value of status in low-performing organizations: Evidence from the video game
industry", *Strategic Organization* **23**(4), 604–629. Published online 2024.
https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/14761270241271021

**What the measure actually is.** Team performance = **revenue generated per team member**, in
money units, **logged** before analysis because the distribution is heavily skewed (video games
is a hit-driven industry).

**Descriptive statistics (Table 1).** Minimum **1.33**, maximum **15,158.12**; a standard
deviation of 962.14.

**Do not do this.** The site previously compared this range against "individual performance
2.63–4.89" and claimed a 15,000× difference. The figures 2.63 and 4.89 **appear nowhere in the
paper**, and a money range cannot be divided by a score range anyway. Removed from the
homepage, the evidence page, an insight article and the references.

**What the paper legitimately supports.** Enormous dispersion in team output across teams doing
comparable work in the same industry — four orders of magnitude. The relationship between team
status and performance is much stronger in organisations with low previous performance.

## 4. Riedl, Kim, Gupta, Malone & Woolley (2021)

"Quantifying collective intelligence in human groups", *PNAS*.
https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2005737118

Meta-analysis of **22 studies, 5,279 individuals in 1,356 groups**. Strong support for a
general collective-intelligence factor. The strongest predictor was **the group's collaboration
process**, which mattered more than the average skill of members. Used on the homepage as a
headline figure (1,356).

## 5. The two papers that challenge the finding

| Source | What it found |
| --- | --- |
| Bates & Gupta (2017), *Intelligence* — "Smart groups of smart people" | Individual IQ accounted for around 80% of the variance in group performance; the effect involving the proportion of women did not replicate. |
| Rowe, Hattie & Munro (2024), *PLOS ONE* — "High-performing teams: Is collective intelligence the answer?" | Failed to replicate Woolley's three predictors; argued for a two-factor model rather than a single factor. |

The site discloses both on the evidence page and in the book's research section. The position
taken: what is settled is that some teams reliably outperform what their members' averages
would predict; the precise measurement is still argued about. The offer's own claim does not
depend on that argument being resolved.

## 6. Katzenbach & Smith (1993)

"The Discipline of Teams", *Harvard Business Review* **71**(2), 111–120. A team is a small
number of people with complementary skills committed to a common purpose, performance goals and
an approach for which they hold themselves mutually accountable. Research base quoted: hundreds
of people, more than 50 teams, 30 companies.

## Where precision is required, and where it is pedantry

Worth flagging, because a reader can open the source and find a contradiction:

- A figure that does not appear in the cited source (the 2.63 and 4.89).
- A comparison between measures that cannot be compared (a money range divided by a score range).
- A finding stated more strongly than the source states it (a headline zero standing in for
  "not strongly correlated").
- A first-party observation presented as though it were a published finding.

Not worth flagging, and not worth correcting in the copy:

- An imprecise duration where the programme's own timeline supports it — "several years" for
  research submitted in 2010, "four years" for research begun in 2011 and published in 2015–16.
- Rounding, such as 700 for 699.
- A figure expressed the way the authors themselves express it.

The test: if an analyst opened the source, would they find a contradiction, or would they find
that the source simply does not specify the point? The first is an error. The second is an
inference, and inferences are allowed. Applying the second test to the first category is how
copy gets weakened for no reason.
