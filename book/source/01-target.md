## PILLAR 1: TARGET

### A Clear Target Related to the Team with a Reward

#### Pillar Introduction

Definition. TARGET is the team's shared line of sight to a valuable result. It is not a wish, a broad corporate ambition, or a task list. A TARGET is clear enough for the team to explain, specific enough to measure, and meaningful enough for people to care about achieving it. The Primary T.A.R.G.E.T Pathway® makes targets Timely, Relatable, Urgent, Explicit, Achievable, Required, Give-oriented, Explainable, and Traceable.

Why it matters. Teams lose energy when they are busy but cannot explain what winning looks like. They also lose trust when every request is declared urgent, when a deadline has no real dependency behind it, or when their work is disconnected from a customer or business need. TARGET restores focus. It lets a team decide what to do now, what to defer, what to stop, and what risks matter most.

Research summary. Google's team-effectiveness research identifies structure and clarity, meaning, and impact as important team dynamics. It found that goals should be specific, challenging, and attainable, while people also need to understand how their work contributes to the organisation's goals.2 The same research distinguishes a genuinely interdependent team from a work group that merely shares information. TARGET creates the shared purpose that makes interdependence useful rather than burdensome.2

Expected outcomes. By the end of this pillar, teams will have a measurable team target, a prioritised value-based backlog, a recognition plan, and a visible risk profile. Team members will be able to explain why the current work matters, how success will be proved, and how their contribution connects to the wider outcome.

### Chapter 1.1: TRUE and EXPLAINABLE — Defining the Destination

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to turn a vague ambition into a target that is timely, relatable, urgent, explicit, and understandable to every team member.

#### Explanation

A team cannot self-organise around a cloudy destination. Consider the instruction, "Improve onboarding." It may sound sensible, but it leaves unanswered questions: Which part of onboarding? For which customer group? What improvement counts? By when? Why does it matter now? When people fill in those blanks differently, they can all work hard while moving in different directions.

The first part of the T.A.R.G.E.T Pathway® corrects this. A TRUE target has an explicit date, is relatable to the team's real work, and contains only genuine urgency. A deadline must represent a real customer commitment, dependency, regulatory requirement, or value opportunity. False deadlines are damaging because they teach people to discount future urgency. An EXPLAINABLE target has an agreed scope and can be understood without translation by the people expected to deliver it.

The practical measurement tool is the T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI). The TVI prevents the team from confusing output with outcome. It asks the team to name the value that should change, the scale used to observe it, the present baseline, the desired target, and the review date. A TVI is not a dashboard decoration; it is an agreement about what success means.

#### Practical Example

A product team receives the statement, "Make the new customer journey better before the launch." In a 30-minute target conversation, the team learns that the commercial objective is to reduce early abandonment in a regulated account-opening journey. They rewrite the target as: "Increase the percentage of eligible customers completing account opening within 24 hours from 42% to 55% by 30 September, while maintaining identity-verification accuracy above 99.5%." The team now has a target that can guide trade-offs, test design, risk discussions, and progress reviews.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — clarity creates coordination. Google found structure and clarity to be one of the dynamics associated with effective teams, including clear expectations and specific, challenging, attainable goals.2 TEAM IQ extends this principle by requiring the team to articulate a measurable stakeholder outcome, not merely a delivery activity.

#### TEAM IQ Measurement: T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI)

The first measurement task is to validate the baseline. If the team cannot reliably establish the current state, it must treat the first cycle as a discovery cycle rather than pretend it can forecast improvement accurately.

#### Exercises and Team Activities

#### Reflection Questions

#### Measurement Task

Within five working days, publish one TVI card for the team's highest-priority outcome. In the next team meeting, ask every member to describe the target without looking at the card. Record the number of accurate explanations out of the number of people present. The team's goal is 100% shared understanding before major work begins.

### Chapter 1.2: ACHIEVABLE and REQUIRED — Balancing Ambition and Value

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to distinguish committed work from non-committed work, connect priorities to real business value, and use the Value-to-Effort Ratio to make transparent trade-offs.

#### Explanation

A target can be inspiring and still be impossible. When leaders repeatedly commit teams to work without involving them in feasibility, the result is not ambition; it is a slow erosion of credibility. The ACHIEVABLE part of TARGET requires the people doing the work to review complexity, uncertainty, capacity, dependencies, and the effort required for quality. It also creates room for work that supports the team itself: learning, coaching, technical health, and collaboration do not happen in the gaps between "real" tasks.

The REQUIRED part asks a harder question: should this work exist at all? A feature with no clear customer, business, risk, or strategic value is not a priority merely because it has been requested. The team should be able to trace it to a target and explain what changes if it is delivered. Doing less is often the art of achieving more.

The Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER) makes that discussion visible. It is not a promise of mathematical certainty; the estimates are judgments. Its purpose is to expose those judgments, compare options using the same logic, and update the choices as new evidence emerges.

VER = Estimated Value Impact ÷ Estimated Cost or Effort

#### Practical Example

A delivery team has capacity for two of four proposed initiatives. Initiative A is a six-week customer-visible enhancement estimated to move the primary TVI by 8 points. Initiative B is a three-day fix expected to move it by 2 points. Initiative C is a four-week architecture change with unclear customer impact. Initiative D is a one-week risk-reduction experiment. The team calculates the VER, discusses confidence levels, and chooses B and D first. The choice is not based on who shouted loudest; it is based on value, effort, and uncertainty.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — impact matters. Google reports that team members' sense that their work is making a difference is important for effective teams.2 A visible value rationale helps teams understand why a piece of work deserves their capacity and gives them permission to challenge work that does not.

#### TEAM IQ Measurement: Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER)

#### Exercises and Team Activities

#### Reflection Questions

#### Measurement Task

Calculate the VER for every item planned for the next work cycle. At the end of the cycle, compare estimated impact with the observed TVI movement. Record where the team over- or under-estimated value. The aim is not perfect forecasts; it is better judgement over time.

### Chapter 1.3: GIVE — The Reward Component

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to build motivation through the G.I.V.E model: Gain, Indicate, Value, and Edify.

#### Explanation

Many organisations ask teams to deliver one target after another while assuming that completing the work should be reward enough. This overlooks a basic human reality: people want to see that their effort matters and that their contribution is recognised. TARGET includes GIVE because a team should not only know what it is trying to achieve; it should know what achievement means for the customer, the business, the team, and the people within it.

GIVE is not a bonus scheme in disguise. It is a culture of edification. Gain makes the win explicit. Indicate shows progress visibly, so effort does not disappear into a black hole. Value connects the result to the customer and organisation. Edify recognises specific contribution, capability, courage, learning, and collaboration. Done well, it avoids the destructive competition created by individual-only reward systems and turns recognition into a force for shared ownership.

#### Practical Example

A service team reaches a target to reduce customer waiting time by 30%. Instead of only reporting the number to senior leaders, the team tells the story of the customer impact, shows the trend line in the service area, recognises the analyst who exposed the bottleneck, the frontline colleague who tested a new handoff, and the technical team that automated part of the process. The recognition is specific, shared, and tied to the outcome rather than generic praise.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — meaning and impact are personal. Google found that meaning can arise from the work itself, the output, supporting the team, or other personally significant sources, while perceived impact helps people see that their work contributes to organisational goals.2 G.I.V.E makes that connection deliberate.

#### TEAM IQ Measurement: G.I.V.E Recognition Signal

The G.I.V.E Recognition Signal is a lightweight team measure that checks whether recognition is connected to real outcomes and shared contribution.

#### Exercises and Team Activities

#### Reflection Questions

#### Measurement Task

For the next two delivery milestones, complete a G.I.V.E Recognition Signal review. Count the proportion of recognition statements that name a specific outcome and observable contribution. The target is that all recognition is evidence-linked and includes both individual and team contribution.

### Chapter 1.4: TRACEABLE — Proving Success and Reducing Uncertainty

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to trace team work to customer or business value and use Target Risk Burn-Down to reduce uncertainty before it becomes expensive rework.

#### Explanation

A target is traceable when the team can follow a clear line from a task, decision, or deliverable to the stakeholder value it supports. Traceability is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It allows a team to make intelligent trade-offs when time is tight. If people understand the value path, they can decide which technical debt can safely wait, which issue is a ticking time bomb, and which feature should be deleted because it is no longer required.

Traceability also means treating risk as a measurable part of delivery rather than an unpleasant list kept for governance. The Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB) identifies the assumptions, dependencies, and obstacles most likely to prevent the TVI target from being achieved. The team assigns each risk an impact and probability score, tests the highest risks early, and re-scores them after learning. The goal is not the illusion of zero risk; it is a steadily safer and more evidence-rich path to the target.

#### Practical Example

In the Architecture versus Development case, monthly review-board meetings created a slow, expensive bottleneck. Development teams moved ahead to meet their dates, then reworked their output after late design decisions. A traceability conversation revealed that the real target was not "complete the review board process" but "make sound architectural decisions in time for delivery." The team embedded architects in delivery teams, created shorter weekly decision reviews, and made dependencies visible. That shifted the work from ceremony to value and substantially reduced rework.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — effective teams review progress in service of a shared project. Google distinguishes interdependent teams from work groups because teams plan work, solve problems, make decisions, and review progress together toward a specific project.2 Traceability and risk reviews give the team a shared basis for those actions.

#### TEAM IQ Measurement: Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB)

#### Exercises and Team Activities

#### Reflection Questions

#### Measurement Task

Maintain the TRB weekly for one active initiative. Report the top three risks, their exposure scores, the latest reduction experiment, and the evidence of change. The team should be able to show not only the current risk profile but also the trend of exposure over time.
