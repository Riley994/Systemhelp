## PILLAR 3: ACCOUNTABILITY

### Know Who Is Doing What

#### Pillar Introduction

Definition. ACCOUNTABILITY is the team's shared certainty about who owns which outcomes, what evidence will demonstrate progress, how handoffs work, and how people will respond when conditions change. It has two levels. Individual accountability means knowing what you have committed to and following through. Team accountability means acting like total football: covering, supporting, escalating, and picking up when the outcome requires it.

Why it matters. Teams become unreliable when roles are vague, work is handed over with unstated assumptions, or people own tasks but nobody owns the result. Equally, accountability becomes corrosive when it is used to blame people for uncertainty, changing conditions, or decisions made without enough information. TEAM IQ accountability is firm about outcomes and humane about learning.

Research summary. Google's research identifies dependability and structure and clarity as important team dynamics. Dependability means team members reliably complete quality work on time; structure and clarity include understanding job expectations, how to fulfil them, and the consequences of performance.2 TEAM IQ turns those insights into shared outcome contracts and visible boundaries.

Expected outcomes. Teams will have named ownership of critical measures, clear outcome commitments, agreed handoff conditions, regular reviews, and a no-blame method for learning from missed commitments.

### Chapter 3.1: Define Roles and Model Accountability

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to distinguish individual from team accountability and create D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contracts that commit people to measurable outcomes rather than task completion.

#### Explanation

Role clarity does not mean putting people in rigid boxes. It means that, at any point, the team can answer: Who is leading this outcome? Who is contributing? Who needs to be consulted? Who needs to know? What counts as complete? Without these answers, teams create duplication, hidden gaps, and the classic end-of-project question: "I thought someone else was doing that."

The D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract (DOC) creates a short, visible commitment. It says what outcome is expected, which metric it should influence, what evidence will prove the result, and when the evidence will be reviewed. This shifts the conversation from "Did you complete the task?" to "Did our work create the expected change, and what have we learned if it did not?"

Leaders must model the standard. When a leader misses a commitment, the right response is not defensiveness. It is to show the evidence, explain the changing condition or flawed assumption, state the learning, and agree the next action. That behaviour grants the team permission to do the same.

#### Practical Example

A team member is assigned "improve login." A DOC rewrites this as: "By the end of the next cycle, I will deliver a login improvement that reduces customer login failures from 8% to 6%, measured by the production error dashboard. If the rate does not reduce by at least one point in the first release, I will present the evidence and propose the next experiment." The individual owns the outcome and the learning, while the team supports dependencies and decision-making.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — dependability is more than being busy. Google defines dependable teams as those in which members reliably complete quality work on time.2 A D.R.E.A.M Outcome Contract makes "quality work" and "on time" evidence-based rather than assumed.

### Chapter 3.2: Regular Check-ins, Empowerment, and Agreements

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to use Quantified D.R.E.A.M Boundaries to assign ownership of key outcomes, design successful handoffs, and empower team members to make decisions inside clear boundaries.

#### Explanation

Accountability fails at the edges between roles. A developer may believe a design has been approved; an architect may believe the developer is still exploring; a product manager may believe customer validation is happening somewhere else. These gaps are rarely caused by laziness. They are caused by boundaries that are implied rather than agreed.

Quantified D.R.E.A.M Boundaries (QDB) maps people and roles to the outcomes that matter. The team places each target, key quality measure, or material risk on a visible board. It then assigns a single primary owner, named contributors, and measurable handoff conditions. A handoff is not "I sent it." It is "The receiving role can use this because the agreed information, quality, and timing conditions are met."

Empowerment follows clarity. A person cannot make a good local decision if they do not know the purpose, boundary, and measures that matter. When these are visible, leaders can give authority nearer to the work without creating chaos.

#### Practical Example

A team repeatedly finds late integration defects between software development and data engineering. QDB reveals that both groups "own" data quality, which in practice means neither owns the end-to-end result. They agree that the data engineering lead owns the data-quality TVI, the software lead owns schema-change notice, and a handoff is complete only when an automated contract test passes and the data dictionary is updated. The next release has fewer late defects because the boundary is now measurable.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — clear structure supports effectiveness. Google notes that team members need to understand job expectations, the process for fulfilling them, and the consequences of performance.2 QDB goes beyond a role chart by connecting expectations to specific measurable outcomes and handoff conditions.
