## Chapter 17 — Deciding where the information is

At 16:20 on a Tuesday, a service team is preparing a change that will alter the way customers reset their passwords. The contact centre has seen a rise in calls after a recent release. A developer has found a narrow change that can be deployed before the evening peak; the service manager knows the likely effect on call handling; the product owner has the latest customer feedback. All three are in the room. The delivery lead says the change must go to the weekly steering group because that is where release decisions are normally confirmed. The steering group meets on Thursday.

By Thursday, the call pattern has moved, the developer is on another item, and the decision is no longer the same decision. The group receives a tidy summary of what was known two days earlier and approves a general direction. The people who could have acted when the evidence was freshest have learned a familiar lesson: notice the problem, assemble the paper, then wait.

### The principle

**INSIGHT** places a sound, timely, evidence-aware decision at the point where information is richest. That does not mean removing leadership or treating every choice as local. It means deciding deliberately which decisions belong close to the customer, system, supplier, or delivery constraint that creates the evidence, and which need a wider authority because the consequence reaches beyond that setting.

Centralised decision making has a legitimate role where a choice changes funding, policy, legal position, customer commitments, or shared risk. The mistake is to use it as a holding area for routine judgement.

The cost is more than decision latency. People closest to the work become good at escalating and poor at deciding. They may stop bringing forward a risk until they have a fully formed case that will survive a senior meeting. They may follow an instruction that no longer fits the situation because changing it would mean another approval route. Senior leaders then spend time making choices for which they have the least current information. Control becomes an appearance of control: a record that someone senior agreed, rather than a record that the best available judgement was made in time.

Purpose-based leadership is the counterweight to uncontrolled local choice. The leader states the outcome to protect and the boundary within which the team may act. A direction such as reducing customer waiting time while protecting service quality and cost gives the team a reason to compare options. It does not prescribe a single action before the team has seen the evidence. The team can then explain its choice through purpose, evidence, and guardrails rather than through rank.

Google’s Project Aristotle identified structure and clarity, meaning, impact, and psychological safety among the dynamics of effective teams. Those conditions support local judgement. People need to know what matters, understand their boundary, see the effect of their work, and be able to say that a concern changes the decision. TEAM IQ turns those conditions into a decision practice; it does not claim that a slogan about autonomy is enough.

D.I.R.E.C.T Value Routing (DVR) provides the practical discipline. It uses the VER, current evidence, and confidence to direct effort towards the highest-value next move. The purpose supplies the direction. The guardrails define the edge of the decision. DVR stops decentralised authority becoming a licence for personal preference, because a team must be able to show why this next action is preferable now.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The password-reset change becomes a chain of requests. The developer asks the delivery lead for approval. The delivery lead asks the product owner for a business case. The product owner asks the service manager for updated call information. Each person prepares a version of the same issue for the next meeting. Nobody has said whether the team is allowed to make a small change that stays within the release boundary.

The steering group finally receives a choice between a short summary and doing nothing. It does not see the discarded options, the details of the service constraint, or the judgement made by the people who investigated it. Members ask for more assurance, partly because the material cannot answer the questions they would ask if they were in the work. The decision returns to the team with a request for a revised paper.

In this arrangement, authority follows the organisation chart rather than the information. A low-risk, reversible action waits beside a decision that genuinely needs senior sponsorship. The team starts to protect itself with email trails. The service manager stops raising small improvements because each one creates a further round of approval. When the incident is later reviewed, the discussion centres on who approved what, not on whether the right person could act when the evidence was available.

### What it looks like when it works

The same team has agreed that it may change guidance and the password-reset route when the work remains within the stated service-quality and cost guardrails. A change that alters customer commitments, creates a material risk, or requires funding outside the agreed work goes upwards. The boundary is visible before the issue appears, so the team is not negotiating authority during the incident.

The developer brings the proposed guide, the service manager brings the current contact pattern, and the product owner brings the customer effect. They use DVR to compare the guide with the larger automation idea and with leaving the route unchanged. The guide is the highest-value next move on the evidence available. The team records why, names the uncertainty, and sets a point to look again at the contact pattern.

The delivery lead does not disappear. Their role is to test whether the purpose and guardrails are clear, to remove a dependency the team cannot remove, and to take a decision upwards when it crosses the agreed boundary. At the next steering group, the team can explain what it decided and what happened. Leaders receive a decision with its reasoning, rather than a request to recreate the reasoning from a distance.

### How to do it

1. **State the purpose before discussing the option.** Write the outcome that must improve and what must remain protected. Keep it close to the work: customer waiting time, service quality, cost, a delivery commitment, or a stated risk. A purpose that only says to complete the planned item gives no help when new evidence suggests a different course. The useful version lets a team compare an action with the outcome it is trying to create.

2. **Set the decision boundary in advance.** Identify which choices the team can make, which choices require consultation, and which require a decision at a wider level. Use the real consequences, not job titles, to draw the line. A choice with a local, reversible effect can sit with the people who have the evidence. A choice that changes a customer promise, creates exposure beyond the team’s agreed guardrails, or commits shared funding belongs at the appropriate wider level. Make the boundary available where the work is planned. If it only exists in a leader’s head, the team will still wait.

3. **Put authority beside the relevant information.** For each material decision, ask where the current evidence sits. It may be with a customer-facing colleague, an engineer who understands the system constraint, a person tracking a supplier, or a product owner holding the current priority. Bring those people into the decision early. Do not ask them merely to supply material for a meeting elsewhere. If the information is distributed, convene the smallest group that can see the whole choice and give that group a named authority within the boundary.

4. **Use D.I.R.E.C.T Value Routing (DVR) to select the next move.** Compare the available actions using the VER, current evidence, and confidence. The discipline matters most when a plan has already absorbed time and effort. DVR gives the team permission to change course when a better option emerges, but requires it to explain why the option is better. In the password example, an automated password-reset guide may have a high VER and low risk, while a broad chatbot replacement has uncertain value and high effort. The next move is not the most ambitious idea. It is the one the evidence supports now.

5. **Make the guardrail part of the decision, not a warning added later.** A team should be able to say what would make its chosen action unacceptable: a fall in service quality, a cost consequence, a risk limit, or a conflict with the stated purpose. Where the guardrail is reached, the team pauses, asks for the right wider authority, or selects another action. This is the responsibility that comes with local authority. It also gives senior leaders a clear basis for intervention without taking every routine choice away from the team.

6. **Record material decisions while their context is still available.** The record need not be lengthy. It needs to preserve the purpose, the option selected, the evidence used, the relevant guardrail, the authority used, and when the team will revisit the decision. This is not paperwork for its own sake. It prevents a later review from becoming a reconstruction based on partial memory and it shows whether the team acted within its boundary.

7. **Review the route, not only the outcome.** Look at whether the team had the relevant information, whether the boundary was clear, whether DVR was used, and whether an avoidable delay was created by escalation.

Purpose-based leadership is demanding for leaders because it requires them to be clear about outcomes and boundaries while allowing the method to be chosen close to the work. It is equally demanding for teams. They cannot say they were following orders when they have been given a decision. That is the point. Good local judgement is visible, reasoned, and connected to the wider purpose.

### The exercise

Run a 55-minute decision-routing session with the people who regularly make or approve delivery choices. Prepare one current decision that has not yet been made, together with the available evidence, any TVI or VER reference, and the existing service, cost, or risk limits. Choose a decision that has enough consequence to matter but is not already committed to a senior forum.

- Spend 10 minutes writing the purpose in one sentence and listing the guardrails. Ask the group to remove instructions disguised as outcomes. Capture the version that tells a team what needs to improve and what must be protected.
- Spend 15 minutes mapping where the relevant information is held. Name the people or roles closest to customers, systems, delivery constraints, and risk. Then identify the smallest group that can make the choice with that information present.
- Spend 15 minutes agreeing the authority boundary. Mark what that group may decide, what it must consult on, and what must go to a wider authority. Capture the specific condition that moves the decision upwards, rather than writing a general instruction to seek approval.
- Spend 10 minutes applying D.I.R.E.C.T Value Routing (DVR) to the available options. Record the VER, current evidence, confidence, the chosen next move, and the guardrail that would cause the team to reconsider.
- Use the final 5 minutes to agree who will record the decision, when the evidence will be reviewed, and which material decisions will be tracked during the next month.

Leave with a one-page decision record and a visible authority boundary. Do not use the session to settle every possible future choice. Its purpose is to make the next real decision faster and more explainable, then learn from the record over the month.

### What to measure

The TEAM IQ measurement is **D.I.R.E.C.T Value Routing (DVR)**. Track material decisions for one month. For each decision, record decision latency from issue recognised to decision made, the decision level, and whether a TVI, VER, or risk reference was used. The artefact is the month’s material-decision record, read at the end of the month by the team and the authority that receives its escalations. Look for avoidable delay: a decision held above the information, a missing reference, or an unclear boundary. Treat the pattern as evidence about the decision route, not as a tally of individual compliance. Compare it with the record from the previous month to see whether the route has improved.

### One thing to do this week

Choose one decision your team routinely sends upwards. Before the next instance, write the purpose, the guardrails, and the condition that would require wider authority. Agree with the relevant leader whether the people holding the current evidence can decide it within those limits. Then use DVR on the next live choice and record what happened.

## Chapter 18 — Interconnected metrics

On the second Friday of a pilot, a team is due to decide whether a new data feature can move beyond its initial users. The product owner wants adoption to grow. The engineer has concerns about accuracy in a narrow set of records. Operations has seen a handful of slow responses during the busiest part of the day. The meeting begins with separate statements: adoption is encouraging, the build is nearly complete, and the response-time issue needs attention.

The decision cannot be made from three good numbers viewed alone. A rise in use has little value if accuracy is not good enough. A technically complete feature is not ready if its response time creates a worse customer experience. Equally, a team can keep improving an already adequate technical measure while losing the customer opportunity it was meant to address. The work needs measures that explain the trade-off between them and a routine that makes the consequence of a change visible before the team simply carries on.

### The principle

Interconnected metrics show how an individual or functional measure contributes to the shared TVI and to agreed quality guardrails. They resist the familiar pressure to optimise the number owned by one role. A development team judged only by speed can create avoidable support cost. A support team judged only by call time can transfer customers rather than solve the underlying issue. Neither group has necessarily behaved badly. They have been given a local measure without a clear connection to the outcome and constraints shared by the wider team.

A wall of numbers does not correct this. It often makes the problem harder to see. Teams can report delivery activity, response time, adoption, defects, cost, and risk in the same pack without agreeing which measures matter together or what decision a movement should prompt. More measures then produce more commentary and less judgement. Interconnection means that a change in one measure has a stated implication for another measure, for the TVI, or for a guardrail. The team can therefore ask a practical question: if we pursue this improvement, what does it do to the outcome we are here to create and to the boundary we have agreed not to cross?

The shared TVI is the anchor. Functional and individual measures should make sense in relation to it, not compete with it. Guardrails make the connection honest. They acknowledge that a team cannot pursue a desired effect at any price. In the data-feature pilot, customer adoption may be the TVI, while accuracy and response time are quality guardrails. The team is not choosing between customer value and technical quality. It is deciding whether adoption is being achieved without breaking the conditions that make the feature worth using.

Measures also need a feedback loop. A measure that is reviewed only after the work is complete cannot guide a live choice. The team needs an agreed cadence to consider what has changed, what has not changed, and what the new evidence means for the plan. That cadence is not a ceremonial update. It is the point at which a team decides whether to continue, change direction, seek a wider decision, or test a different idea.

Insight Risk Triggers (IRT) make the feedback loop explicit. An IRT is a predefined threshold connected to a date or point in the work. If a risk remains above an agreed exposure by that date, or if a test has failed to produce evidence by that point, the team must pause, pivot, escalate, or choose a new experiment. The trigger prevents a weak but common habit: recognising that the evidence is against the plan, then continuing because there is no agreed moment to decide otherwise.

One local number is rarely enough to explain whether the work is helping. TEAM IQ connects the measures the team already needs so that it can see trade-offs and act while there is still a choice.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The pilot team reviews progress through separate updates. Product reports that adoption is moving in the right direction. Engineering reports that the planned work is complete. Operations reports that response time has worsened at busy periods. Each update is accepted. The meeting ends with three actions owned by three people and a plan to review them next week.

No one has connected the response-time change to the customer-adoption aim. No one has said what level of accuracy is necessary before the feature should be extended. The team has not agreed what evidence would stop a broad rollout. Because the measures are separate, every issue can be treated as another item to improve while the main decision is deferred.

The feature is extended because the completion measure is green and adoption has increased. Later, the team spends time responding to accuracy concerns that were already visible. Operations feels that its warning was noted but not used. Engineering feels that a difficult technical constraint was made secondary to a growth target. Product believes it had a clear reason to proceed. All three accounts are understandable, and all point to the same gap: the measures did not explain their relationship or require a decision.

### What it looks like when it works

Before the pilot starts, the team states customer adoption as the TVI and names accuracy and response time as guardrails. It discusses how the measures relate. Adoption that rises while accuracy remains below the agreed condition is not a reason to scale. A response-time deterioration that affects the customer experience is not a technical footnote. The team has one view of the decision, even though colleagues remain responsible for different evidence.

It also agrees an Insight Risk Trigger (IRT): if accuracy cannot exceed 97% by the end of the second pilot week, the team will not scale the feature. The condition is visible to everyone who is doing the work. It is not introduced after the threshold has been missed as an explanation for a decision that has already become difficult.

At the Friday meeting, accuracy has not reached the trigger. The team does not argue about whether the incomplete result should be described as good or bad. It follows the agreed response and pivots to a narrower use case. Product can explain the customer effect, engineering can focus on the condition that needs more evidence, and operations can test response time in the smaller setting. The decision is timely because the team agreed in advance what the evidence would mean.

### How to do it

1. **Start with the shared TVI.** For a significant initiative, name the outcome measure that tells the team whether the work is creating the intended effect. The TVI is the reference point for choosing supporting measures. If the team cannot identify the shared TVI, it is not ready to use activity or completion as a substitute.

2. **List the measures held by the different parts of the work.** Bring together the measures used by product, delivery, operations, engineering, service, or other relevant colleagues. Include the measures that affect the customer outcome and the measures that show whether the work is staying within agreed quality guardrails. Do not begin by building a large reporting pack. The task is to identify the small set that could pull the team in different directions if read alone.

3. **Describe the relationship between each measure and the TVI.** For every measure, write what a movement means for the shared outcome or a guardrail. A measure may support the TVI directly, warn of a cost of pursuing it, or test whether a stated assumption holds. In the data-feature example, adoption is connected to the intended customer effect; accuracy and response time test whether that effect is usable. If a measure has no clear relationship to a decision, question why it is being reviewed in this forum.

4. **Agree the guardrails before a target creates pressure.** State the conditions the team will protect while pursuing the TVI. Quality guardrails stop a locally attractive result from hiding a wider cost. Make them specific enough that the team can recognise a breach. Where there is uncertainty, record what evidence is still needed. The aim is not false precision. It is to avoid discovering after a decision that different roles were protecting different boundaries.

5. **Create at least two Insight Risk Triggers (IRT) before delivery begins.** Each IRT should state the evidence or risk exposure, the threshold, the date or delivery point, and the required decision window. An IRT can cover a risk that remains above agreed exposure by a given date, or a test that has not produced evidence by a given point. State the response in advance: pause, pivot, escalate, or choose a new experiment. The response may vary, but it must be a decision rather than a vague request to monitor the position.

6. **Set a feedback-loop cadence that matches the work.** Agree when the team will read the interconnected measures together and who needs to be present to interpret them. At each review, ask what changed, what did not change, and what the evidence now means for the plan. A regular session with no decision need can be short. A trigger review needs the authority and evidence necessary to act within the stated decision window. Do not let the meeting become a sequential round of updates; return repeatedly to the TVI, guardrails, and IRTs.

7. **Respond to a trigger as a test of the feedback loop.** When an IRT is reached, make the agreed choice and record the time taken. If the team decides late, analyse why. Perhaps the data was unavailable, the right people were not in the review, the boundary was unclear, or a colleague did not feel able to report the issue. Do not dismiss the delay as merely a delivery problem. It is evidence that the feedback loop failed to turn information into a decision.

8. **Remove measures that do not help the decision.** After several reviews, ask whether each measure has explained a trade-off, protected a guardrail, or prompted an action. If not, it may belong elsewhere or not be needed. Interconnected metrics make the cost of a trade-off visible early enough for the team to choose deliberately.

### The exercise

Run a 60-minute IRT design session before the next significant initiative begins. Prepare the proposed TVI, the evidence already available, the likely quality guardrails, and the people responsible for the measures that will inform the decision. Keep the focus on the evidence that will guide a pivot-or-persevere choice.

- Spend 10 minutes agreeing the shared TVI and writing the outcome it represents. Capture the quality guardrails that must be protected while the team pursues that outcome.
- Spend 15 minutes listing the relevant individual, team, and organisational measures. For each, state how a movement affects the TVI or a guardrail. Put aside measures that do not explain a current decision.
- Spend 20 minutes creating at least two Insight Risk Triggers (IRT). For each one, record the threshold, the date or point in the work, the evidence or risk exposure it concerns, the required decision window, and whether the response will be to pause, pivot, escalate, or choose a new experiment.
- Spend 10 minutes agreeing the feedback-loop cadence and the people who must be present when an IRT is read. Make clear where the linked measures will be viewed together.
- Use the final 5 minutes to test one trigger against a plausible result. Ask whether the group could make the stated decision with the evidence named. If not, revise the trigger before delivery starts.

Capture a single page linking the TVI, guardrails, supporting measures, and the two IRTs. Share it with everyone who supplies or uses the evidence. Its purpose is to make an agreed future decision easier, not to predict every result.

### What to measure

The TEAM IQ measurement is **Insight Risk Triggers (IRT)**. For the next significant initiative, create at least two IRTs before delivery begins and read them on the agreed cadence with the measures that explain them. The artefact is the IRT record: the threshold, timing point, decision window, and the action taken when it was reached. Measure whether trigger decisions occur within the stated decision window. A late decision should be analysed as a feedback-loop failure, not simply a delivery failure. Review the record after the initiative to see whether the triggers exposed the right trade-offs. Compare the timing with the stated decision window and capture the reason whenever the team acts after it, including missing evidence, unclear authority, or unavailable data.

### One thing to do this week

For one active initiative, put its TVI and two quality guardrails on the same page. Ask the team to name one measure that could look good while damaging one of those guardrails. Agree one Insight Risk Trigger (IRT) with a threshold, date, and decision window. Read it at the next review alongside the linked measures.

## Chapter 19 — Clear channels

At 08:45 on release day, a security concern is raised while checks are under way. The release manager has heard it in a private message from an engineer. A delivery lead recalls a related risk from the previous week’s meeting. The product owner has an email with a customer commitment, and the person who owns the service-quality guardrail is travelling. By 09:20, fragments of the issue sit in different places. Nobody can point to a decision record.

The release is delayed. That may be the right choice. Three months later, a new manager asks why it was delayed and whether the condition still applies. The answer should not depend on who happens to remember the morning. If the organisation cannot find the context of a material decision, it has not shared the information in a form that can be used. It has merely passed messages around at speed.

### The principle

Clear channels and transparent information sharing give people a usable route to operational updates, evidence, decisions, risk escalation, and learning. Transparency does not mean broadcasting everything to everyone. A person cannot make better choices because they receive every message in the organisation. They need relevant information that they can find, understand, and use in time. That is a practical standard. It asks whether the right audience can locate the current item, see what it means, and know what action or authority it requires.

A channel needs a clear purpose: what belongs there, who keeps it current, and when it is read. The test is the pathway from information to action; a meeting or shared workspace that cannot be used in time has failed.

When information is scattered across private conversations, hidden spreadsheets, and meeting memories, the organisation becomes dependent on gatekeepers. A delivery lead may have to translate between the engineer who knows the system, the product owner who knows the customer effect, and the leader who holds the decision boundary. Each translation can remove a condition, an assumption, or a warning. People who are not present may act on an old version because they have no clear source for the current position. This is not a problem solved by copying more people into email. It requires a recognised channel for the particular kind of information.

D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audits (DDA) turn transparency into learning. A DDA asks whether the team followed a sound decision process: was the purpose clear, did it seek evidence, were assumptions and risks visible, was the VER considered when appropriate, and was the decision recorded? The audit does not punish a decision because the outcome was uncertain. Complex delivery includes choices where the result cannot be known in advance. The purpose of the audit is to improve the quality and traceability of the next choice.

Google’s Project Aristotle notes that people in psychologically safe teams can bring up problems and difficult issues, ask for help, and take interpersonal risks. Clear channels are the operational infrastructure that makes that behaviour usable. Someone may be willing to raise a security concern, but the concern still needs a route that reaches the people with the evidence and authority in time. Safety without a route can leave important information private. A route without safety can leave it empty.

Transparency therefore has a boundary. It shares the context necessary for the relevant people to understand and use a decision. It makes material choices traceable without turning every working conversation into a public record. It lets a new colleague understand why a release was delayed, what alternatives were considered, who made the call, and when the decision should be reviewed. That is enough to reduce dependence on memory and enough to support a better decision next time.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The security concern moves through direct messages, a hurried call, and a meeting that has no agreed record. The engineer tells the release manager about the technical condition. The release manager tells the delivery lead that there is a risk. The product owner hears only that the release may be delayed. By the time a senior leader is asked for a decision, the description has become a choice between delaying and accepting an unspecified concern.

After the release is delayed, one colleague updates a private spreadsheet and another adds a brief line to meeting notes. A team member who was absent believes the delay was caused by a capacity issue and begins discussing a different response. The original security concern is no longer visible enough to guide the work.

The next time a similar concern is raised, people hesitate. They do not know whether to post it in the operational update, take it to a meeting, send it to a named leader, or create a risk entry. They choose the least exposed route: a private conversation. Information then sits with the people who happened to receive it. The organisation has communication activity but no clear channel for a material decision.

### What it looks like when it works

The team has already identified pathways for operational updates, evidence, decisions, risk escalation, and learning. The engineer records the security evidence in the agreed place and uses the risk-escalation route because the release condition is affected. The release manager brings together the people who hold the relevant information and authority. The operational update points to the current decision record rather than trying to reproduce it in several messages.

The record states the context, risk evidence, TVI guardrails, alternative options, who made the decision, and the planned review. It also makes clear that the release is delayed because the security condition must be understood before the team can proceed. People who need to act can find the same current position. People who only need to know the release status receive an update that tells them what has changed without exposing every working detail. At the next D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audit (DDA), the team checks whether the evidence reached the decision makers in time and whether the record made the choice understandable. The delay is not treated as proof of failure or success on its own; the process supplies learning for the next choice and its channel.

### How to do it

1. **Name the information pathways that delivery actually uses.** Start with the five types in the work: operational updates, evidence, decisions, risk escalation, and learning. Describe the route for each in plain terms. Where does a colleague put a current operational update? Where is evidence for a material choice held? How is a risk made visible to the right authority? Where is a decision recorded, and where does the team review what it learned? This exposes gaps hidden by the vague instruction to keep people informed.

2. **Give each channel a purpose and an audience.** State what belongs in the channel, who needs to read it, who keeps it current, and when it is used. The channel for an operational update may direct people to a decision record rather than become the record itself. Evidence may need to be available to the people analysing an option. A material decision needs an identifiable record that survives a meeting. Do not create several locations that claim to be the current source. If a message points elsewhere, make the destination clear.

3. **Create a decision record for material choices.** Use a decision log that captures the context, risk evidence, TVI guardrails, alternative options, who made the decision, and the planned review. Add the purpose, the authority used, the assumptions and risks made visible, and whether the VER was considered when appropriate. These are not decorative fields. Each allows someone who was not in the room to understand how the choice was made and whether a later change in conditions should alter it.

4. **Make relevant information findable in time.** Test the channel with the people who need it, not just the person who created it. Can an engineer find the current customer constraint? Can a product owner locate the risk evidence? Can a leader see the decision that needs their authority without reading every operational discussion? Do not rely on a colleague knowing whom to ask. A clear channel reduces the number of gatekeepers between an item of evidence and the decision it should inform.

5. **Separate visibility from indiscriminate distribution.** Transparent sharing is selective enough to keep information useful. The people affected by a release delay may need the decision and its consequence; the people conducting the technical investigation may need fuller evidence. Set access and updates according to what people need to understand or act. When a decision changes, update the record first and use the operational channel to direct readers to it. 

6. **Use risk escalation as a decision route, not a parking place.** A risk channel should make clear what exposure is being raised, what evidence supports it, which guardrail is affected, and who has authority to act. Give the escalation a required response point. If an issue is not material, keep it in the operational path.

7. **Run D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audits (DDA) on a sample of records.** Audit the process rather than judging the outcome with hindsight. Ask whether the purpose was clear, evidence was sought, assumptions and risks were visible, the VER was considered when appropriate, the authority was clear, the decision was recorded, and a review date was present. A poor outcome may still show a good process in uncertain work. A favourable outcome may reveal weak practice that will fail when conditions change.

8. **Use the audit finding to improve one channel at a time.** If decision records have evidence but no clear authority, improve that field and the route into the authority. If risks are visible but decisions have no review date, correct the record and the review routine. Choose the weakest field as the next improvement focus. Avoid a large communications redesign in response to a small, observable gap. Use channels people will follow under delivery pressure.

### The exercise

Run a 55-minute clear-channels session with the delivery lead, people who supply operational and risk information, and the authority that receives material escalations. Prepare one recent material decision or a live decision that is likely to be made soon. Bring the evidence available, the current decision record if one exists, and access to the places where the team currently shares information.

- Spend 10 minutes listing the team’s paths for operational updates, evidence, decisions, risk escalation, and learning. Capture the current location or meeting for each path and identify where the group is relying on private messages or memory.
- Spend 15 minutes complete a decision record for the chosen item. Include context, purpose, risk evidence, TVI guardrails, alternative options, authority, assumptions and risks, whether the VER was considered when appropriate, who made the decision, and the planned review.
- Spend 10 minutes test findability. Give each participant a different question about the item and ask them to locate the information through the agreed channels. Note where a person has to ask a gatekeeper or finds two competing versions.
- Spend 15 minutes conduct a short D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audit (DDA). Check the record for clear purpose, evidence, authority, and review date. Agree one weakness in the pathway or record, and assign the person who will correct it.
- Use the final 5 minutes to set the four-week trial: log every material decision using the DDA fields, then audit a sample of five at the end of the period.

Capture the pathway list, the completed decision record, and the single improvement focus. The session should not decide which technology everyone must use. It should make the route and the required information clear enough that a material decision can be understood after the meeting ends.

### What to measure

The TEAM IQ measurement is **D.I.R.E.C.T Decision Audits (DDA)**. For four weeks, log every material decision using the DDA fields. At the end of the period, audit a sample of five records. Report the proportion with clear purpose, evidence, authority, and review date. The artefact is the decision log and its DDA sample, read at the end of each four-week period by the team and the relevant decision authority. Choose the weakest field as the next improvement focus. A record is useful because it shows where information ceased to be usable, not because it proves a decision was certain. Compare proportions with the previous audit.
