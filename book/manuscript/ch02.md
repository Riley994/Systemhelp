## Chapter 2 — Output is not value

The monthly delivery review starts well. The team has a full status pack, a release calendar, a burn-up chart and a page of completed work. The delivery lead has highlighted the items that went live, the tickets that were closed and the documents that passed review. The room can see that the team has been busy and that work has moved through its process.

The person funding the work listens, then asks their portfolio lead to say what is different outside the team as a result. There is a pause. Someone points to the release notes. Someone else explains that a dependency was removed and that the backlog is healthier. Those things may be useful, but they do not tell the funder whether a customer can now do something they could not do before, whether a business cost has fallen, or whether a material risk has changed.

The meeting turns into a defence of effort. The team has done what it said it would do. The funder has not received an account of what their money changed. Both statements can be true, which is why this conversation so often ends with more reporting rather than a better decision.

### The principle

**Output** is the work a team produces: a release, a closed ticket, a set of story points, a design, a policy, a test result or a document. It is visible because the team controls most of it. Output matters. You need to know whether work is moving and whether technical standards are being met. It is not, on its own, a reason to continue funding a team.

**Value** is a meaningful change for someone who cares about the result. That person may be a customer, a colleague handling an operational task, a regulator, a business owner or the organisation that carries the cost and risk. Value is not a feature list with a better label. It is the difference the feature, service or decision makes when it is used.

A senior reader will recognise the distinction. A team may release a self-service form. The output is the form and the integration behind it. The value may be that a customer completes a request without calling the contact centre, that the contact-centre colleague has fewer avoidable contacts, or that a request is completed more accurately. A team may also close a large batch of security findings. The output is the closed findings. The value is lower exposure to an identified risk, provided the closure is real and the remaining risk is understood.

The value question is plain: for whom will something change, what will change and how will you know? Add timing and cost to create and sustain it. Until a team can answer, it cannot be measured in a way that settles a performance conversation. Every available measure will describe activity: items started, items completed, time booked, tests run or pages produced. Those measures can be accurate and still leave the main question unanswered.

That is why arguments about performance become unwinnable. One leader points to delivery volume; another to an unchanged business result; finance to cost. Each has a partial view, but none shares a defined result against which the team can be judged. The argument is then conducted through confidence, seniority and slide quality.

Google's team-effectiveness research cautions that measures such as lines of code and bugs fixed can mislead when used alone. The point is not to discard operational measures. It is to put them in their proper place: as evidence and guardrails around the result that matters. A team needs enough evidence to show that it delivered the right value, to the right standard, at the right cost and at the right time.

### What it looks like when it is missing

In the next review, the same team returns with a fuller dashboard. The programme office has added separate counts for releases, defects, test cases, approvals and work in progress. The delivery lead can show that the queue is moving faster, but no one has agreed which customer or business result that movement should serve.

Work is selected because it is ready, because a senior stakeholder asked for it, or because it will make the plan look healthier. A feature reaches production and is treated as complete at deployment. Support receives new questions, operations changes a manual work-around and customers find another route, but those signals sit in different meetings. The team has no reason to bring them together because the work item is already closed.

Cost is discussed only when a request needs approval. The build estimate is compared with the remaining budget, while maintenance, training, support, security and retirement are treated as someone else's concern. When the outcome is weak, the response is to ask for more features, faster delivery or a more detailed plan. No one can show whether that extra activity is likely to improve the result. No one owns the connection between delivery data and the result the work was meant to change.

### What it looks like when it works

The team enters the review with a short statement at the top of its pack. It names the people affected, the change they should experience, the evidence that will show it and the date by which the team expects to learn. Its delivery measures are still there, but they are no longer the headline.

For an internal service, the team might state that people making a particular request should complete it without rekeying information, and that the organisation will see fewer avoidable contacts and less manual handling. The team brings task-completion evidence, operational data and a technical guardrail. If completion improves but service demand does not, the team investigates rather than declaring victory. If a release causes a reliability issue, the guardrail makes that visible even if the customer measure initially improves.

The funder can now ask useful questions. Is this the change we still need? Is the evidence credible? What will it take to achieve and sustain it? What should stop if this becomes the priority? The conversation moves from whether the team worked hard to whether continuing the work is a sound use of capacity. The statement stays open until the relevant evidence has been read and acted on.

### How to do it

Use this method for a live workstream where its effects can be observed. It is a practical TEAM IQ method, not a claim that a sentence creates value. Its purpose is to give the team and funder a shared result to test.

1. **Choose one workstream.** Select a live stream, not the whole portfolio or a single technical task. Bring its objective, operating data and the person who can explain its funding. One stream prevents a vague statement that tries to cover everything.

2. **Name the person who must experience the change.** Write down the customer, operational colleague, business owner or other affected group. Describe their present situation in observable terms. Do not begin with the feature. Begin with the friction, cost, delay, risk or missed opportunity the work is intended to change. If the team cannot name a group and a current condition, pause. It has a delivery request, not yet a value case.

3. **Write the change and the evidence.** Use this form: For [named group], change [current condition] to [desired condition] by [timeframe], shown by [evidence], while maintaining [necessary guardrail]. Make every field specific enough that another leader could disagree with it. A statement that says an experience will be better is not testable. A statement that says a named group can complete a named task with fewer avoidable contacts, shown through service data and task evidence, can be tested.

4. **Set the threshold and the cost view.** Apply the 7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE) before development begins. Define what good enough means for the customer result and the relevant quality requirements, such as accessibility, reliability, accuracy, security, performance or maintainability. Record the guardrails that cannot be traded away. Then make the unit economics visible. A delivery team costs more than build effort: include people, contractors where used, platforms, licences, supplier commitments, support, operations, security, training, maintenance and retirement. Bring costs held elsewhere into the conversation.

   The board-level view is value per team. Set the value achieved in the period against the full cost of keeping that team available for the period, and identify costs that the change creates, removes or avoids over its life. Do not turn uncertain effects into a precise financial claim. State the assumptions, distinguish evidence from expectation and revise the view as use and operating data arrive. A cheap build that creates recurring support work may be poor value; a larger initial outlay may be justified where it removes recurring cost or material harm.

5. **Test the statement before committing more capacity.** Ask a leader who did not write it to explain the promised change. Ask the funding owner whether the change would alter a priority decision. Ask people closest to delivery whether evidence can be collected without a new reporting programme. Finally, ask what result would show that the work should be stopped, changed or not extended. If the team cannot answer, rewrite the statement. Make uncertainty part of the decision.

6. **Review results, not just release status.** Build a 7 C's Outcome Metrics (COM) scorecard around the statement: at least one customer measure, one business measure and one technical guardrail. Review the evidence after release and at a cadence that suits the decision. Use it to scale, improve, reposition or retire the work. Retain delivery measures, but read them as leading evidence, not as a declaration of value.

### The exercise

Run this as a fifty-five-minute session with the manager, delivery team and, where possible, funding owner. Prepare one active workstream, its objective and any customer, service, finance or operational evidence. Do not prepare a polished business case. Expose what is known and assumed.

1. **Set the workstream and affected group — 10 minutes.** The manager names the stream and group expected to benefit. Members write the current condition independently: what happens now, where the friction is and who bears the consequence. Compare descriptions and agree one that can be observed.

2. **Draft the value statement — 15 minutes.** Use the form in the method: group, current condition, desired condition, timeframe, evidence and guardrail. Read it aloud. Remove feature names unless needed to describe the change. Capture unresolved assumptions beside the draft.

3. **Check cost and quality — 15 minutes.** Use the 7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE) to identify the good-enough threshold and guardrails. List lifecycle costs that could change: build, support, operations, security, training, maintenance and retirement. Mark what is known, what needs validation and who owns it.

4. **Run the board test — 10 minutes.** One person who did not write the statement explains the value, the evidence and the cost trade-off without looking at the draft. The group then names the result that would cause it to stop, change or lose priority. If the explanation is unclear, revise the statement once.

5. **Close with ownership — 5 minutes.** Capture the statement, evidence sources, guardrails, lifecycle-cost assumptions and next review date. Assign one owner for evidence collection and one for the funding conversation. The manager sends the record before the next delivery review.

The output is not a promise to produce a particular feature. It is a testable account of why the work deserves capacity and what the team will learn.

### What to measure

The primary artefact is a value statement supported by a 7 C's Outcome Metrics (COM) scorecard. The statement records the affected group, the intended change, the evidence, the timeframe, the guardrail and the lifecycle-cost assumptions. The scorecard holds at least one customer measure, one business measure and one technical guardrail, so the team does not replace one narrow output count with another.

Use the 7 C's Value Engineering Standard (CVE) to record the agreed good-enough threshold before work begins. Where the work is substantial, add a Lifecycle Value Review to bring support, operations, finance, security and product perspectives into the cost view. Read the statement and scorecard in the normal delivery review, then read the outcome evidence at the decision cadence set by the workstream. The funding owner should see the same record, not a separate account of progress.

### One thing to do this week

Choose the workstream that consumes the most attention in your next delivery review. Before the meeting, ask its funding owner to complete one sentence: the group whose situation should change, the change they expect and the evidence they would accept. Put that sentence at the top of the status pack. If nobody can write it clearly, make defining it the first item of work rather than adding another output chart.
