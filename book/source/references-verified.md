## Verified from the publication

**1. Woolley, A. W., Chabris, C. F., Pentland, A., Hashmi, N., & Malone, T. W. (2010).**
"Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups."
*Science*, 330(6004), 686–688. DOI 10.1126/science.1193147.

- Source: `Science_JournelCollectiveIntelligence2010-2.pdf` (4 pages).
- The study: **699 people in groups of two to five**, across two studies — not 700, and
  not a two-year study. Anywhere the site or the book says 700 or two years, correct it.
- The finding: a general collective intelligence factor ("c factor") that predicts group
  performance across a wide variety of tasks. It is **not strongly correlated with the
  average or maximum individual intelligence** of members. It **is** correlated with
  (a) average social sensitivity of members, (b) equality in the distribution of
  conversational turn-taking, and (c) the proportion of females in the group.

**2. Szatmari, B., Deichmann, D., & van den Ende, J. (2025).** "The increasing value of
status in low-performing organizations: Evidence from the video game industry."
*Strategic Organization*, 23(4), 604–629. Published online 2024. DOI 10.1177/14761270241271021.

- Source: the SAGE PDF (26 pages). This is the article behind the SAGE link in the
  evidence register.
- The finding: the link between a team's status and its performance is much stronger in
  organisations with **low previous performance**; in organisations that have performed
  well recently, status matters much less, because there is no expected resource scarcity
  pushing decision makers towards high-status teams.

**3. Katzenbach, J. R., & Smith, D. K. (1993).** "The Discipline of Teams."
*Harvard Business Review*, 71(2), March–April 1993, 111–120.

- Source: `HarvardReviewTheDisciplineofTeams1993.docx`.
- The finding: a team is a small number of people with complementary skills, committed to
  a common purpose, a set of performance goals and an approach for which they hold
  themselves mutually accountable. The distinction between a team and a working group
  turns on performance: a working group relies on individual contributions, a team
  produces something greater than its members could achieve individually.
- The research base quoted: hundreds of people, more than 50 teams, 30 companies.

**4. Google (n.d.).** "Understand team effectiveness." *re:Work* guide, Project Aristotle.
https://rework.withgoogle.com/en/guides/understanding-team-effectiveness

- Source: `GoogleTeams2025.docx` (the guide's own text) and two accompanying documents,
  `GooglePsychologicalSafety.docx` and `Googleanalysisform.docx` (the psychological-safety
  guidance and the Team Effectiveness Discussion Guide).
- The finding: five dynamics of effective teams — psychological safety, dependability,
  structure and clarity, meaning, and impact. Psychological safety was the most important
  of the five. The research ran at Google from 2011 for about four years.

**5. Tovey, M. (ed.) (2008).** *Collective Intelligence: Creating a Prosperous World at
Peace.* Oakton, Virginia: Earth Intelligence Network. Foreword by Yochai Benkler.

- Source: `CollectiveIntelligence_022508-CFINAL1420.pdf` (648 pages).
- Note: this is **not** the MIT study. It is a separate edited collection on collective
  intelligence. Cite it only if the book makes a claim that rests on it.

**6. Burkus, D.** "What Makes Some Teams High Performing?" *Harvard Business Review.*

- Source: `HarvardreviewKeyfactorsforHigh-performingTeamsDavidBurkus.docx`.
- Caution: the supplied file reads as a summary of the article rather than the article
  itself, and it contains no date, volume or page numbers. Cite it as an HBR article
  only if the user confirms the piece; otherwise leave it out. It is not load-bearing
  for any claim in the book.
