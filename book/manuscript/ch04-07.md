## Chapter 4 — True and explainable

The launch meeting has reached the last slide. A sponsor points at a coloured mock-up and says that account opening needs improving before launch. The product lead writes it into the plan. The compliance lead assumes the problem is the identity check. Operations hears a request to shorten the time between application and activation. The engineers begin discussing the screens that are hardest to change. Each person has taken away a reasonable interpretation. None has taken away the same assignment.

By Friday, there is activity everywhere. A designer has changed the welcome screens. An analyst has pulled a list of abandoned applications. A technical lead has asked about a third-party dependency. The status report says the work is under way. It cannot say whether the work will improve the result that prompted it, because nobody has agreed what that result is. The team has been given a direction, not a destination.

### The principle

A **TARGET** is a team’s shared line of sight to a valuable result. It is not a wish, a broad corporate ambition or a list of activity. It tells people what must change, why it matters, and how they will know it has changed. That distinction is easy to lose when a programme is moving quickly. “Improve onboarding” sounds useful until a team tries to decide which item should be done first, which compromise is acceptable and which risk can wait.

The Primary T.A.R.G.E.T Pathway® begins by making a target TRUE and EXPLAINABLE. TRUE means **Timely, Relatable, Urgent and Explicit**. There is a real date, connected to the team’s work. The reason for urgency is real as well: a customer commitment, a dependency, a regulatory requirement or a value opportunity. A date copied from a steering-paper template is not urgency. It is a signal that people will learn to disregard, along with the next genuinely urgent request.

Relatable matters because teams cannot act on a target that lives only in senior language. A customer outcome, an operational change or a risk reduction has to connect to work the team can see and influence. Explicit means that scope has boundaries. It says what is included, what is not, and what counts as enough. Timely does not mean hurried. It means that the date has a reason the people doing the work can repeat.

EXPLAINABLE is the test that exposes whether those elements have been done properly. A target is explainable when a person in the team can describe it without translating it into their own specialist version. They should be able to say the outcome, the stakeholder, the measure, the intended change and the date. If several answers sound sensible but differ, the target is still cloudy. The resulting disagreements will surface later as rework, delayed decisions or a dispute about whether the team has finished.

Google’s Project Aristotle identified structure and clarity, including clear expectations and specific, challenging and attainable goals, as a dynamic of effective teams. TEAM IQ takes that finding into delivery practice: the team agrees a measurable stakeholder outcome rather than merely accepting a delivery activity.

The T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI) makes the agreement visible. It records the value that should change, the scale used to observe it, the present baseline, the desired target and the review date. Its purpose is not to make a report look more managed. It is to prevent output from being mistaken for outcome. A completed screen, a signed specification or a released feature may matter, but none alone proves that the value has moved.

A team without this discipline can be extremely busy and still be unable to state what winning looks like. That is expensive. It fills meetings with local opinions, allows each specialist to optimise their own slice, and makes a missed date look like a delivery problem when the original problem was a target nobody could properly explain.

### What it looks like when it is missing

Back in the account-opening work, the delivery plan contains “improve the process” as a heading. There is a launch date, but no one can say whether it represents a customer promise, a dependency or simply the date chosen for the plan. The product lead asks for a shorter form. Compliance asks for more evidence. Operations asks for fewer contacts. The engineers hear all three requests as in scope.

At the weekly review, progress is described in completed tasks: screens built, test cases drafted, a supplier meeting held. The commercial sponsor asks whether more eligible customers will complete the process. The room goes quiet, then someone promises to find a number. A baseline has not been agreed, so the number cannot be compared with anything useful.

The team then protects itself with broad language. Every item is urgent because nobody has separated the real deadline from the noise around it. A change that would help one audience is accepted because no agreed scope says otherwise. People work hard, but they are coordinating around interpretations. The first time the gap becomes obvious is when a late decision forces work to be changed. The missing agreement means risks remain personal concerns until they become a programme problem.

### What it looks like when it works

The same team pauses before adding work to the plan. It establishes that the commercial concern is early abandonment by eligible customers in the regulated account-opening process. It agrees which part of the process it can influence, what completion means, how the result will be observed and the date on which the result will be reviewed. Identity-verification accuracy is recorded as a condition that must not be damaged while the outcome improves.

The target is written in ordinary language and read back by the product, compliance, operations and technical leads. The wording survives the test: each can state the same result and the same boundary. The designer can now decide whether a proposed screen change supports completion. The analyst knows which baseline to validate. The technical lead can judge whether a dependency threatens the date or merely affects a lower-value option.

At the weekly review, the team talks about movement in the measure and what it has learned, not only what it has produced. A request that does not support the target is deferred with an explanation. A risk to identity-verification accuracy is treated as a decision about the target, not as a technical detail to hide in a separate meeting. The work becomes easier to coordinate because the destination is stable enough to guide choices.

### How to do it

1. **Start with the outcome, not the requested activity.** Put the current request on the table exactly as it was received. Then ask the people closest to the customer, operation, risk or commercial need to state what should be different if the work succeeds. Keep pressing until the answer describes a change in value rather than a deliverable. “Release the new form” is output. “More eligible customers complete account opening within the intended period” is an outcome that can be tested.

2. **Make the target TRUE.** Agree the date and write down why it is the date. A real reason can be a customer commitment, a dependency, a regulatory requirement or a value opportunity. If no reason can be found, do not invent urgency to make the plan look decisive. Identify the team’s actual area of influence so the target is relatable. Set clear scope boundaries. This is where a team distinguishes the essential outcome from worthwhile but separate improvements.

3. **Make the target EXPLAINABLE.** Write one sentence that includes the stakeholder outcome, the measure, the intended change and the review date. Use language that a delivery manager, a service colleague and a technical specialist can all repeat. Remove internal shorthand that only one function understands. Do not settle for nods around the table. Ask each person to say the target in their own words. Where the accounts differ, improve the wording rather than assuming the difference does not matter.

4. **Create the T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI).** Record five things: the value that should change; the scale used to observe it; the present baseline; the desired target; and the review date. Keep the TVI close to the work, rather than filing it with a monthly pack. It should be visible when the team makes a trade-off. A short card is often enough, provided every field is complete and understandable.

5. **Validate the baseline before forecasting improvement.** Teams are often under pressure to promise a result before they know the current state. Do not turn a weak measure into false precision. If the baseline cannot be reliably established, say that the first cycle is a discovery cycle. The team can still make useful work visible, but it cannot honestly forecast the scale of improvement until it has evidence of where it starts.

6. **Use the target as the filter for delivery decisions.** Bring the TVI into refinement, planning, design reviews and progress conversations. For each significant item, ask how it helps move the stated value, what condition it must protect and whether it fits the agreed scope. This changes the quality of disagreement. Instead of arguing from a function’s preference, people can argue from the shared destination. When circumstances change, revise the target openly and record why; do not allow separate informal versions to emerge.

7. **Keep urgency credible.** A new request may be genuinely urgent, but it should have to show the same evidence as the original target. If it displaces planned work, name what it displaces and what value is delayed. This is not resistance for its own sake. It protects the team from being asked to treat every instruction as a crisis and protects the real deadline when one exists.

The method is deliberately plain. Its strength is not in clever wording. Its strength is that the team has one account of the result, one way of observing it and one date with a reason behind it. That gives people permission to stop, challenge and choose without waiting for someone outside the room to interpret the target for them.

### The exercise

Run a target-definition session for the team’s highest-priority outcome. Prepare the current request, any known customer or business need, the available data for a baseline, and the relevant date or dependency. Give the group a copy of the current wording, even if it is vague. The point is to expose what it leaves unresolved rather than to pretend that it was clear all along.

Spend the first ten minutes asking what value must change and for whom. Capture competing interpretations without trying to settle them too quickly. Spend the next fifteen minutes agreeing what makes the target Timely, Relatable, Urgent and Explicit. Write the reason for the date, the scope boundary and the condition that must be maintained. Use the next fifteen minutes to draft the sentence that makes the target EXPLAINABLE, then ask every participant to repeat it without reading the draft.

Use the final ten minutes to complete one T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI): value, scale, baseline, desired target and review date. If the baseline is unreliable, record that the first cycle is a discovery cycle. Within five working days, publish the TVI card. In the next team meeting, ask every member to describe the target without looking at the card. Record the number of accurate explanations out of the number of people present. Capture any difference in wording as work to fix, not as a personal failure. Keep the card in the working area, not only in the meeting record, for later use.

### What to measure

The artefact is the **T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI)** card for the highest-priority outcome. It holds the value to change, the scale, current baseline, desired target and review date. Read it when the team plans work, reviews progress and makes a material trade-off. In the next team meeting, measure shared understanding by recording accurate explanations against people present. The goal is **100% shared understanding** before major work begins. If that standard is not met, improve the target and repeat the explanation test rather than treating the card as complete. A failed explanation test is evidence that the wording, scope or measure still needs work before delivery becomes costly for the people who must carry it out.

### One thing to do this week

Choose the work item currently described with the broadest phrase on your plan. Book a short conversation with the people who interpret it differently. Do not discuss the solution first. Agree the stakeholder outcome, the measure, the baseline and the reason for the date. Write one TVI card and ask each person in the room to explain it without reading it.

## Chapter 5 — Achievable and required

The quarterly planning board is full before the meeting starts. A sponsor has marked every request as a priority. A regulatory change has a date. Sales wants a commitment for a customer. The platform lead has added an architectural concern. A team member has put coaching and technical health at the bottom of the list, under a heading called “if time allows”. The delivery manager is asked to confirm that the team can take it all.

Nobody in the room wants to be the person who says no. The requests are all plausible. Each has an advocate with a reason to care. So the list is accepted with caveats, which means it is accepted. Work begins before the team has discussed capacity, uncertainty, dependencies or what quality will require. By the middle of the cycle, the team will be called slow. In fact, it was asked to commit to more work than a credible plan could contain.

### The principle

A clear target can still fail if it is not achievable. Ambition is useful when it sharpens a team’s choices and harmful when it asks people to pretend that complexity, uncertainty and capacity do not exist. The ACHIEVABLE part of the Primary T.A.R.G.E.T Pathway® requires the people doing the work to review those realities before a commitment is made. It also requires an honest view of effort needed for quality, rather than treating quality as something to negotiate away once the date becomes uncomfortable.

Achievable does not mean cautious or low ambition. It means that a commitment has been tested by the team that must deliver it. They consider the work itself, the dependencies around it, the gaps in what is known, the capacity available and the effort required to do it properly. Where evidence is missing, they make the uncertainty visible. That creates a plan that can be managed. A forced promise creates a plan that needs constant explanation.

There is another discipline inside the target: REQUIRED. A request is not a priority merely because somebody has requested it, or because it has reached a senior forum. The team should be able to trace every significant item to a customer, business, risk or strategic value and explain what will change if it is delivered. Work without that rationale may be desirable. It is not automatically required.

This matters because capacity is not limited only by development work. Learning, coaching, technical health and collaboration support the team’s ability to meet the target. They do not happen in the gaps between supposedly real tasks. When leaders plan as though they do, the first pressure point removes the very work that keeps future delivery possible.

The Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER) makes these conversations visible. Its calculation is simple:

VER = Estimated Value Impact ÷ Estimated Cost or Effort

The number is not a promise of mathematical certainty. Both parts are estimates, and estimates are judgements. The value of the Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER) is that it makes those judgements discussable. Options can be compared using the same logic. Confidence can be challenged. A team can update its choices as evidence changes instead of defending the first list because it appeared in a plan.

Google’s Project Aristotle found that a sense that work is making a difference is important for effective teams. A visible value rationale does more than motivate. It gives people grounds to challenge work that consumes capacity without helping the target, and it gives a delivery manager a clear explanation for deferring it.

The cost of skipping this step is predictable. Teams are loaded with committed and non-committed work without distinction. Their best people spend time recovering from interruptions and late discoveries. Progress is reported as activity because nobody can explain the value case for the queue. The organisation then concludes that delivery needs more pressure, when it needs better choices.

### What it looks like when it is missing

In the planning meeting, every request is called essential. Nobody has written the customer, business, risk or strategic value alongside it. The architectural concern is treated as an optional inconvenience because its value is not described. Coaching is dismissed as overhead. The team is given a date before it has seen a dependency that could determine whether the work is possible.

The plan does not distinguish what the team has committed to from what it might do if circumstances allow. A stakeholder hears a discussion item and reports it as promised. Another adds a new item because it sounds small. The delivery manager carries the resulting confusion into the weekly status report, where red, amber and green labels hide the fact that the team has no agreed basis for choosing.

When a problem appears, the response is to work harder on everything. Quality checks are compressed. Learning is postponed. The people doing the work know which requests have weak value, but no common measure gives them permission to say so. The loudest request wins, not because it is most valuable, but because it arrived with more authority. The team is then judged against commitments it never had enough evidence or capacity to make.

### What it looks like when it works

The same board starts with the T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI) for the active outcome. Each proposed item is placed beside that outcome and given a stated value case. The team marks whether it is a commitment for the next cycle or work that may be considered later. A request with no clear link is not quietly smuggled into the queue. It is returned for a clearer rationale or deferred.

For the remaining options, the people who will do the work discuss complexity, uncertainty, capacity, dependencies and the effort required for quality. They also reserve room for learning, coaching, technical health and collaboration. An architectural change may still be selected because it reduces a material risk to the target. A customer-visible enhancement may wait because its value case is weak or its assumptions need testing.

The team calculates the Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER) and records the judgement behind it. The ratio does not decide for them. It gives them a common starting point and exposes where confidence is low. The decision is published with what the team will do, what it will not do yet and why. A sponsor may disagree, but the disagreement is now about value, effort and uncertainty, rather than an accusation that delivery lacks commitment.

### How to do it

1. **Make the upcoming work visible as choices.** Gather every item proposed for the next work cycle in one place. Include requests from sponsors, operational concerns, technical work, learning, coaching and collaboration. Do not start by arranging them into a delivery sequence. First distinguish between work that is being proposed and work the team is genuinely able to commit to. This avoids the familiar mistake in which a list of possibilities is heard as a list of promises.

2. **Connect each item to value.** For every item, state the customer, business, risk or strategic value it is expected to affect. Then connect it to the relevant target and its TVI. If nobody can say what changes when the item is delivered, it has not met the REQUIRED test. That does not make the request foolish. It makes it unsuitable for priority status until its reason is clearer. Capture the unanswered question and give it back to the person best placed to answer it.

3. **Test ACHIEVABLE with the people who do the work.** Ask about complexity, uncertainty, capacity, dependencies and the effort needed for quality. Take each answer seriously. A dependency is not covered by writing “dependency” in a plan; the team needs to understand how it affects the target and when it will be resolved. Uncertainty is not failure. It is a reason to adjust a commitment, sequence a learning activity or test an assumption before committing further.

4. **Calculate the Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER).** Estimate value impact and estimated cost or effort for each item, then use the same calculation across the list. Discuss the confidence behind each estimate. A ratio based on a weak assumption should not be presented as a fact. The purpose is to compare choices consistently and reveal where the team needs more evidence. Keep the calculation alongside a short explanation, because a bare number can conceal the judgement that produced it.

5. **Protect work that sustains delivery.** Put learning, coaching, technical health and collaboration into the capacity discussion before the queue is full. These are not rewards for completing the programme work. They are part of the conditions under which the team can complete it. Where there is no capacity for all proposed work, make the trade-off explicit. Defer an item, reduce its scope or revisit its date; do not simply erase the work that makes reliable delivery possible.

6. **Choose, publish and revisit.** Select the items with the strongest combination of value, effort and evidence for the next cycle. State what is committed and what is not. Record why a lower-VER item was selected if a risk, dependency or other known condition justifies it. That preserves an honest decision trail. At the end of the cycle, compare the estimated impact with observed TVI movement. The objective is not perfect forecasts. It is better judgement over time.

7. **Use new evidence to change the queue.** A priority decision made at planning is not permanent. If an assumption is disproved, a dependency changes or the TVI shows an unexpected movement, revisit the VER. Explain the change in the same terms used for the original choice. Teams lose trust when work disappears without a reason, but they gain trust when a visible value case is updated by evidence rather than by hierarchy.

The practical discipline is to make less work more honest. A team that has named its commitments can coordinate around them. A team that has named the value of its options can decline or defer work without making the discussion personal. That is how ambition becomes credible rather than merely loud.

### The exercise

Run a prioritisation session for the next work cycle. Prepare the full list of proposed items, the relevant T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI), any available evidence of customer, business, risk or strategic value, and the team’s known capacity and dependencies. Ask the people who will deliver the work to attend; a Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER) agreed without their view of effort is only a management preference with a calculation attached.

Use the first ten minutes to separate committed work from non-committed work. In the next fifteen minutes, ask the group to state the value case for each item and its link to the target. Mark items whose rationale cannot yet be explained. Spend the following twenty minutes discussing complexity, uncertainty, capacity, dependencies and quality, and estimating value impact and cost or effort. Calculate the VER for every item planned for the next work cycle. Capture confidence and assumptions beside the calculation.

Use the final ten minutes to select the planned work and record what has been deferred, stopped or sent back for a clearer value case. At the end of the cycle, compare estimated impact with the observed TVI movement. Record where the team over- or under-estimated value. Do not use the review to assign blame for an estimate. Use it to identify which assumptions were sound, which were weak and what evidence should be sought earlier next time. Keep the reasoning visible so a changed priority can be tested against the original judgement when evidence changes.

### What to measure

The measure is the **Value-to-Effort Ratio (VER)** for every item planned for the next work cycle. The artefact is a prioritised list that shows estimated value impact, estimated cost or effort, the ratio, its confidence and its connection to the TVI. Read it when work is selected and again at the end of the cycle. Compare estimated impact with observed TVI movement, then record over- and under-estimates. The measure is useful because it improves judgement over time; it is not a claim that estimates can remove uncertainty. Read the list with the team, not only in a sponsor meeting, so the people carrying the commitment can challenge an estimate before it becomes a promise and creates avoidable pressure in delivery.

### One thing to do this week

Take the next request added to your team’s plan. Before accepting it, ask the requester to state the customer, business, risk or strategic value and the TVI it supports. Ask the team what effort, dependency and uncertainty sit behind it. Calculate a provisional VER beside the existing work. If the item displaces something, name what will move and why. Write the answer beside the request.

## Chapter 6 — Give

A service team has met the target it has carried for months. The waiting-time trend has moved in the right direction. Customers are getting through the service more quickly. At the review, the senior leader thanks the room, reads the number from a slide and moves on to the next issue. The slide is filed. The analyst who found the bottleneck goes back to a queue of work. The frontline colleague who tried a different hand-off hears nothing. The technical team that changed the slowest step sees only another request arrive.

Nothing hostile happened. The team was thanked. Yet the moment has told people that the effort disappears once the result reaches a report. It has not connected the work to the people helped by it. It has not shown how the result was achieved. It has not made visible the behaviour worth repeating. Over time, a team can learn that targets are a conveyor belt: finish one, receive a general acknowledgement, pick up the next.

### The principle

TARGET includes GIVE because a team needs more than a destination. People need to see what achievement means for the customer, the business, the team and the people who contributed. The G.I.V.E model is **Gain, Indicate, Value and Edify**. It is a practical way to make reward and recognition part of delivery, rather than an occasional speech after the useful lessons have already been lost.

GIVE is not a bonus scheme in disguise. It is not generic praise, a points system or an individual contest dressed up as appreciation. It is a culture of edification: making specific contribution visible in a way that strengthens shared ownership. This matters in work where outcomes depend on people handing work to one another, raising a difficult risk, learning a new skill or testing an idea that may not work. Individual-only reward systems can make those behaviours less likely by turning colleagues into competitors.

**Gain** makes the win explicit. The team should be able to say what changed and what has been achieved. **Indicate** shows progress visibly, so effort does not vanish into a black hole between one reporting cycle and the next. **Value** connects the result to the customer and organisation. It explains why the change was worth making. **Edify** recognises observable contribution: capability, courage, learning, collaboration and the specific actions that helped the team reach the outcome.

The order matters. Recognition detached from the result is easy to give but weak. It sounds kind, but it does not teach the team what value looked like or why a colleague’s action made a difference. A shared result without recognition is also incomplete. The team sees a number improve, but not the contributions and behaviours that deserve attention next time.

Google’s Project Aristotle found that meaning can come from the work itself, the output, supporting the team or other personally significant sources, while perceived impact helps people see how their work contributes to organisational goals. G.I.V.E makes that link deliberate. It does not assume that every person is motivated by the same thing. It gives the team enough evidence to connect effort, outcome and contribution without making claims about what should matter to any individual.

The absence of GIVE has a practical cost. Progress becomes a management artefact rather than a team event. People notice that invisible work is rarely recognised, so they may choose more visible activity over necessary support. A colleague who raises a risk can be seen as slowing things down rather than protecting the target. Teams then repeat the mistake of treating motivation as private sentiment, when part of it is shaped by what the organisation chooses to notice in public.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The waiting-time result is announced as a number. The story stops there. There is no account of the customer experience that changed, no indication of the progress made during the work and no explanation of which decisions made the difference. A colleague who stayed with a difficult investigation is thanked in the same phrase as everyone else. A team member who tested an unproven hand-off is not mentioned because the contribution was less visible than a release.

The next delivery milestone becomes a race for personal visibility. People keep useful knowledge close because only the person who presents the final result is noticed. Help given across roles is absorbed into “team effort” with no detail. That may sound collective, but it gives the team no example of the behaviour it needs more of.

At the next review, the leader tries to correct this with louder praise. The praise is still detached from outcome and contribution. It does not tell the team what was gained, how progress was indicated, what value was created or what should be edified. The words are positive but leave no usable memory. The result may be repeated, but the team has learned little about how to repeat it.

### What it looks like when it works

The service team marks the result by making the Gain clear: the agreed waiting-time outcome has been reached. It indicates the path, showing the movement in the service area so people can see that progress was earned through several changes, not produced by a single announcement. It then states the Value in ordinary language: customers wait less, and the organisation has removed a source of avoidable delay.

Edification is specific. The analyst is recognised for exposing the bottleneck. A frontline colleague is recognised for testing a new hand-off in live work. The technical team is recognised for changing part of the process that was slowing the service. The team is recognised for combining those contributions and keeping the shared outcome in view. Nobody is presented as a lone hero, and nobody is hidden inside a vague “well done”.

The recognition includes learning as well as a completed outcome. A failed test that ruled out an attractive but unhelpful route can be edified when it reduced uncertainty and helped the team choose better. A difficult risk raised early can be edified when it protected the customer result. The team sees that contribution is not limited to the most visible task. It is linked to evidence, to the target and to the way the team worked together.

### How to do it

1. **Set the reward component when the target is agreed.** Do not wait until a milestone has passed and then search for something pleasant to say. Alongside the target, agree what a meaningful Gain would be, how progress will be Indicated, what customer or organisational Value will be stated, and which forms of contribution should be Edified. This is a recognition plan, not a script. It makes the team alert to evidence while the work is happening.

2. **Name the Gain precisely.** Use the target and its TVI to state what changed. Avoid treating effort alone as the win. A difficult period may deserve acknowledgement, but it should not be confused with the result the team set out to achieve. If the outcome has not yet moved, recognise useful progress and learning honestly rather than declaring success early. This protects both morale and trust in the measure.

3. **Indicate progress where the team can see it.** Make the movement toward the target visible in the normal places where work is discussed. The point is not to create another dashboard. It is to prevent progress, blocked work and evidence from disappearing into a status report that few people read. Show what was tried, what changed and what remains uncertain. Visible indication allows a colleague’s contribution to be linked to a real moment in the work rather than remembered only by the most senior person.

4. **State the Value beyond the metric.** Translate the result into its customer and organisational meaning. A reduced delay may mean less waiting for a customer. A risk reduction may mean greater confidence in a regulated process. A clearer hand-off may mean fewer avoidable contacts. Keep the claim proportionate to what the team can prove. The purpose is to connect the result to a real stakeholder, not to write a grand narrative around a modest change.

5. **Edify observable contribution.** Name the person or group, what they did and how it supported the outcome. Recognise capability, courage, learning and collaboration when there is evidence for each. “Thank you for the support” is a gesture. “You exposed the bottleneck, which allowed the team to test the hand-off that improved the service result” is useful recognition. It tells the person that their work was seen and tells others what behaviour helped.

6. **Recognise both individual and team contribution.** Teams need both. An individual contribution can be specific without suggesting that the outcome was achieved alone. Team recognition can be collective without flattening the work into a generic phrase. In practice, describe the individual action and the hand-offs or combined decisions that made it effective. This reduces the destructive competition created by recognition that rewards only personal visibility.

7. **Include learning and constructive challenge.** A recognition culture that notices only successful delivery will make people hide uncertainty. Edify the person who raised a hard dependency early, the group that stopped a weak idea, or the colleague who shared a capability that improved the team’s decision. Connect the recognition to the evidence and the target. GIVE should encourage ownership of the outcome, not just celebration when the result is already safe.

8. **Review the signal, not the enthusiasm.** At a milestone, check whether recognition statements name a specific outcome and observable contribution. If they do not, improve the practice. The aim is not more applause. The aim is recognition that helps the team understand what it achieved, why it mattered and how collective work produced it.

GIVE works because it makes the social part of delivery concrete. Teams see the line from target to customer value, and from contribution to result. That is more durable than a reward event because it makes useful behaviour visible while there is still another decision to make.

### The exercise

Prepare a G.I.V.E review for an upcoming delivery milestone. Bring the relevant TVI, the available progress evidence, and short notes on contributions that have helped or hindered the path to the outcome. Ask participants to arrive with examples rather than names alone. The exercise is not a popularity discussion; it is a check that recognition is connected to real outcomes and shared contribution.

Spend ten minutes agreeing the Gain the team is seeking and the evidence that will show it. Use the next ten minutes to decide how progress will be Indicated visibly during the milestone. Spend fifteen minutes translating the target into its customer and organisational Value. Keep the language specific enough that a new team member would understand why the work matters. In the next fifteen minutes, identify contribution that can be Edified: capability, courage, learning and collaboration, with an observable action for each.

Use the final ten minutes to write the recognition statements the team will use when the evidence is available. For the next two delivery milestones, complete a G.I.V.E Recognition Signal review. Count the proportion of recognition statements that name a specific outcome and observable contribution. Capture whether both individual and team contribution are included. The target is that all recognition is evidence-linked and includes both individual and team contribution. Where a statement is generic, rewrite it against the outcome rather than adding more praise. Keep the statements with the milestone evidence so the review can be repeated consistently.

### What to measure

The **G.I.V.E Recognition Signal** is the team’s lightweight check on whether recognition connects to a real outcome and shared contribution. Its artefact is the set of recognition statements reviewed at each of the next two delivery milestones, with the stated outcome and observable contribution shown beside each. Count the proportion that meet both tests and read the result immediately after the milestone. The target is that all recognition is evidence-linked and includes both individual and team contribution. Treat a weak signal as a prompt to improve specificity, not to make recognition performative. Use the signal to refine the next milestone’s recognition plan while the work and evidence are still fresh for people directly involved in that work.

### One thing to do this week

At your next team review, select one contribution that would normally disappear into a general thank-you. ## Chapter 7 — Traceable

The monthly architecture review has become a fixture in the delivery calendar. A development team brings a design, waits for the meeting, receives late questions and carries the answers into the following month. The date on the plan does not move, so development continues. When an architectural decision eventually arrives, work is changed to fit it. The status report shows milestones and approvals. It does not show the cost of waiting, the assumptions being made or the value that the review process was meant to protect.

In one of these meetings, the discussion centres on whether the team has completed the review-board process. That sounds like a reasonable governance question. It is the wrong target. The team needs sound architectural decisions in time for delivery. The review board is only one possible means of achieving that. When a process becomes detached from the result it serves, people can comply with it and still create delay, uncertainty and rework.

### The principle

A target is **TRACEABLE** when the team can follow a clear line from a task, decision or deliverable to the stakeholder value it supports. That line makes work intelligible under pressure. When time is tight, the team can decide which technical debt can safely wait, which issue threatens the outcome and which feature should be deleted because it is no longer required. Without it, every item competes on local urgency and every function can claim that its own work must come first.

Traceability is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It does not require a large administrative system. It requires a shared explanation of why work exists and what part of the target it affects. A developer should be able to connect a design choice to the value path. An analyst should be able to connect a process change to the stakeholder result. A delivery manager should be able to explain why a dependency matters before it turns into a missed commitment.

The Primary T.A.R.G.E.T Pathway® also treats risk as part of delivery rather than an unpleasant list kept for governance. The Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB) identifies the assumptions, dependencies and obstacles most likely to prevent the TVI target from being achieved. The team assigns each risk an impact and probability score, tests the highest risks early, and re-scores them after learning. It is a route to evidence, not an attempt to pretend that uncertainty has vanished.

The goal is not zero risk. A team that reports no risk in complex work may simply have stopped looking. The goal is a steadily safer and more evidence-rich path to the target. Each reduction experiment should tell the team something material: whether an assumption holds, whether a dependency can be met, whether a proposed approach protects the stakeholder outcome, or whether the target needs revisiting.

Google’s Project Aristotle distinguishes an interdependent team from a work group because teams plan work, solve problems, make decisions and review progress together toward a specific project. Traceability and risk review give the team a shared basis for those actions. They replace a collection of specialist updates with a conversation about the same outcome.

When traceability is absent, rework often appears to be a technical failure or a people failure. In reality, a team was asked to make decisions without seeing the value path and the uncertainty around it. A late question arrives as a surprise because the dependency was not connected to the target. A feature remains in scope because nobody can show why it is no longer required. The bill is paid in waiting, changed work and damaged confidence.

### What it looks like when it is missing

In the architecture review, the team measures progress by whether a paper has reached the board. Development carries on because the delivery date is fixed. Architects hold questions until the next formal meeting because that is how the process works. By the time a decision is made, a design has been built around an assumption no one tested. The subsequent change is described as rework, but the status report records only that the approval was completed.

The risk list is maintained separately. It has assumptions, dependencies and obstacles, but they are not connected to the TVI or to the decisions in the delivery plan. Items are discussed when a governance forum asks for them, not when they are most likely to become expensive. Impact and probability scores are copied forward. There is little evidence that the team has tried to reduce the highest exposure.

A request to remove a low-value feature starts an argument because the feature has become part of the plan. Nobody can state the stakeholder value it supports, but nobody feels authorised to delete it. Meanwhile, a dependency that could affect the target is treated as someone else’s problem. Work continues around it until the uncertainty becomes a delay.

### What it looks like when it works

The team restates the real result: sound architectural decisions in time for delivery. It maps the active work, decisions and dependencies to the stakeholder value and the TVI. The review-board process is then assessed against that result. Where the monthly meeting creates delay without improving decision quality, architects are embedded with delivery teams and shorter weekly decision reviews are used. Dependencies are made visible where planning happens.

Each material assumption, dependency and obstacle is entered into the Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB) with impact and probability scores. The team does not wait for the full design to be complete. It tests the highest risks early. A decision review can then use evidence from the experiment, not only opinion. After the learning, the scores are revisited and the exposure trend is visible.

When a feature no longer supports the value path, the team can remove it with a clear explanation. When technical work protects a condition required by the target, it can be prioritised without pretending that it is merely an internal preference. The group has fewer arguments about whose work matters most because it can see the line from the work to the outcome. It is not free of uncertainty; it is dealing with uncertainty while choices are still affordable.

### How to do it

1. **Begin at the target and the TVI.** Put the current stakeholder outcome, the value measure, baseline, desired target and review date where the team can use them. Traceability starts with a result worth tracing to. If the TVI is unclear, repair it before constructing a detailed account of the work beneath it. A precise list connected to a vague target only creates precise confusion.

2. **Draw the line from work to value.** For each material task, decision and deliverable, state the stakeholder value it supports and the part of the target it affects. Keep the statement short enough to use in planning. Where the line cannot be drawn, ask whether the work is required, whether it is supporting a condition that must be protected, or whether it should be removed. This is the point at which low-value features and hidden assumptions become visible.

3. **Make dependencies part of the value path.** A dependency is not external background. It changes the team’s ability to achieve the target. State what must happen, who or what it depends on, when the uncertainty becomes material and what stakeholder value is at risk. Bring it into the same discussion as scope and sequencing. This enables a team to act earlier than a forum that sees the issue only when it has become a late exception.

4. **Build the Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB).** Identify the assumptions, dependencies and obstacles most likely to prevent the TVI target being achieved. Give each risk an impact and probability score. Use a consistent team scale and record the reasoning, so a score can be challenged with evidence rather than treated as a private judgement. The TRB is not a catalogue of every possible problem. It is a focused view of the uncertainty that matters to the target.

5. **Test the highest risks early.** Select the risks with the greatest exposure and agree a reduction experiment for each. An experiment may test an assumption, clarify a dependency or produce evidence on an obstacle. Make the intended learning explicit before the test begins. A meeting that simply repeats concern does not reduce uncertainty. After the learning, re-score the risk and record the evidence that changed the assessment.

6. **Use the TRB in weekly delivery decisions.** Review the top risks, their exposure scores, the latest reduction experiment and evidence of change. Ask which planned item is affected by the current risk profile and whether scope, sequencing or a decision needs to change. If a risk cannot yet be reduced, make that fact visible and explain its effect on the target. This is more useful than reporting a nominally green plan that depends on untested assumptions.

7. **Challenge process by the result it serves.** A review, approval or document may be necessary, but it should be judged by whether it helps the team make sound decisions in time for delivery. If a process produces late learning, find a shorter feedback route that preserves the needed decision quality. The aim is not to discard governance. It is to connect governance to stakeholder value, evidence and the target.

8. **Preserve the decision trail.** When work is added, removed or changed, record the trace to value and the evidence behind the decision. This gives new team members and sponsors a reliable explanation of why the queue looks as it does. It also prevents a previous decision being defended merely because it is old. Traceability should make it easier to change course when evidence demands it.

A traceable target changes the team’s relationship with uncertainty. Risks are no longer an administrative burden and dependencies are no longer someone else’s issue. Both become part of the work of protecting a valuable result. That is what allows a team to move quickly without pretending that speed removes the need for thought.

### The exercise

Run an initial Target Risk Burn-Down session for one active initiative. Prepare the current T.A.R.G.E.T Value Index (TVI), the list of active work and decisions, known dependencies, and any existing risks or assumptions. Ask the people who hold delivery, operational, technical and stakeholder knowledge to attend. The purpose is to create a shared risk profile connected to the target, not to transfer risk ownership into a document.

Spend the first ten minutes restating the target and identifying the line from active work to stakeholder value. Use the next fifteen minutes to list the assumptions, dependencies and obstacles most likely to prevent the TVI target being achieved. In the following fifteen minutes, assign impact and probability scores and agree the highest exposure risks. Use the final ten minutes to choose a reduction experiment for each of the highest risks and state the evidence that would justify a re-score.

Maintain the TRB weekly for the active initiative. Report the top three risks, their exposure scores, the latest reduction experiment and the evidence of change. Capture the trend of exposure over time, not only the current profile. If a risk has not changed, record why and what decision is required. If a test has reduced uncertainty, re-score it openly. The exercise succeeds when the team can show how it is making the path to the target safer and more evidence-rich. Keep the current profile visible beside the previous review so the team can discuss direction as well as urgency.

### What to measure

The **Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB)** is the artefact and the measure. It records the assumptions, dependencies and obstacles most likely to prevent the TVI target, with impact and probability scores, reduction experiments and evidence from each review. Read and update it weekly for one active initiative. Report the top three risks, their exposure scores, the latest reduction experiment and the evidence of change. The useful measure is the trend of exposure over time: it shows whether the team is reducing uncertainty before it becomes expensive rework. Use the weekly view in delivery conversations, so the people making choices can see which uncertainty has changed and which still needs a reduction experiment before it threatens the target or forces rework later.
