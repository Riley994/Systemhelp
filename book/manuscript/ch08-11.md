## Chapter 8 — Create safety and lead by example

At 08:40 on the morning of a release, the test analyst opens the deployment checklist and finds that the rollback instruction points to an old environment. The delivery manager is already on a call with the operations lead. A product owner is asking when the customer notice can go out. The analyst has checked the link twice. It is wrong, but raising it will delay a release that has been described as straightforward all week.

For a moment, nothing happens. The analyst drafts a chat message, deletes it, and looks again at the checklist. In teams without safety, this is where a small defect becomes a later incident. People wait for certainty beyond what the work can offer, or decide that the most senior person must already know. By the time the issue is raised, the choice is no longer between a short pause and a correction. It is between a difficult recovery and an explanation of why nobody spoke when there was still time.

### The principle

**Psychological safety** is the confidence that you can take an interpersonal risk at work without being embarrassed or punished. In delivery terms, that means admitting that you do not know, asking a basic question, reporting a mistake, challenging a premise, or saying that an estimate has become unreliable. It does not remove standards. It makes it possible to see whether the standards can be met before a status report has turned a concern into a surprise.

Google's Project Aristotle places psychological safety first among its listed team dynamics. Its definition is practical: teammates do not embarrass or punish one another for admitting mistakes, asking questions or offering ideas. The Massachusetts Institute of Technology collective-intelligence studies also associate collective intelligence with social sensitivity and a more equal distribution of conversational turn-taking. Neither finding says that politeness produces delivery. Both point to a condition for good judgement: the people closest to a risk must be able to put it into the work.

Safety is often misunderstood as a promise that every suggestion will be accepted or every decision will be comfortable. It is neither. A safe team can stop a weak proposal, test an uncertain claim, set a demanding date, and hold an owner to an agreement. The difference is that it deals with the work rather than making the person who raised the issue the problem. “What are we seeing, what does it mean, and what do we need to do next?” is a better first response to bad news than a search for somebody to blame.

Leaders set this condition in ordinary moments. A leader who becomes defensive when challenged teaches the team to keep its doubts private. One who publicly dismisses an idea teaches people to edit themselves before they speak. A leader who says, “I may be missing something — what do you see?” makes room for information that their role does not give them. A leader who names a wrong call and asks what can be learned makes fallibility discussable rather than dangerous.

The C.L.E.A.R.L.Y Evidence Standard (CES) gives that behaviour a place to land. It moves a claim out of personality and into one of four forms: an assumption, a hypothesis, an evidence statement, or a test plan. The CES asks whether evidence is available, whether an assumption is recorded, whether an experiment is defined if needed, and whether learning is captured. A challenge can then be directed at the claim, not at the status of the person making it.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The analyst eventually sends a private message to a colleague: “This link may be old.” The colleague replies that the operations lead probably knows. On the call, the delivery manager says the release is ready because that is what the last update said. Nobody wants to be the person who reopens the plan.

The release proceeds. When rollback is needed, the team discovers the error under pressure. The first questions concern who changed the checklist and why it was not found sooner. The analyst says that the link had looked wrong that morning, then wishes they had said so earlier. The room hears a confession rather than useful evidence. The next person who spots an uncertainty will remember that exchange.

You can see the same pattern in planning. A senior stakeholder says that customers will definitely prefer a new approval route. The statement enters the plan as fact. A quieter team member who has no customer evidence stays silent. The team now carries an untested preference as if it were a requirement, and discussion shifts to defending the date built on it.

The claim gains weight with every plan that repeats it. Soon, challenging it feels like challenging the stakeholder rather than checking a condition that matters to the work.

### What it looks like when it works

The analyst posts the concern where the release team can see it: the rollback link is old, the current instruction has not been verified, and the team needs ten minutes to check it. The delivery manager thanks them for catching it before deployment and asks the operations lead to validate the route. The customer notice waits. Nobody pretends that the pause is convenient; the point is that it is visible and manageable.

The team corrects the checklist, records what was learned, and continues. Later, the manager asks what in the review method allowed an obsolete link through. That question is not soft. It seeks a change in the work, not a performance of regret.

In the approval-route discussion, the leader responds to the quiet challenge by saying that the team is assuming customer preference. The group records the assumption and agrees to run a prototype test with ten customers before committing. The stakeholder's view remains useful as a hypothesis. It no longer has to win because of who said it. This is leadership by example: curiosity is shown when the cost of curiosity is a slower decision. The record distinguishes experience from customer evidence. That distinction allows the team to proceed with a visible test rather than pretending that a confident view has settled the matter.

### How to do it

1. **Make the expected response to bad news explicit.** Say at the start of planning, review and delivery work that concerns, uncertainty and mistakes are inputs to the work. Use a consistent first response: establish what has been observed, what is known, what is assumed, and what needs to happen next. You are not asking people to be fearless. You are making the route for raising a concern clear enough to use under pressure.

2. **Model uncertainty before asking others for it.** A leader can say, “I may be missing something,” before giving a view; ask what evidence supports the current position; and state plainly when a previous call needs revisiting. Do this in front of the people affected, not only in a private conversation with trusted colleagues. The team watches what happens to dissent in public. It also notices whether the leader leaves enough time for an answer rather than supplying the answer immediately.

3. **Separate a claim from its owner.** When somebody says, “This will definitely work,” do not answer with an argument about confidence or seniority. Ask which form the claim is in. Is it an evidence statement? Is it an assumption that needs recording? Is it a hypothesis that could be checked? Or does it need a test plan? The language is deliberately ordinary. Its value is that it makes uncertainty visible without treating it as incompetence.

4. **Use the CES at the point of commitment.** Before work is approved, a decision is made, or a delivery promise is repeated, check the four conditions: evidence available, assumption recorded, experiment defined if needed, and learning captured. Not every claim will meet all four immediately. A claim with no evidence may still be necessary to move work forward, but it should be recorded as an assumption with a next step rather than dressed up as certainty.

5. **Welcome the useful challenge in the moment.** Thank the person who notices the missing condition, then direct the group to the work. “What evidence do we have?” and “What would test this?” are stronger than “Are you sure?” because they request something the team can act on. If you disagree, explain the reasoning and leave the challenge visible. A polite dismissal has the same long-term effect as an angry one.

6. **Close the learning loop.** When an experiment finishes or an assumption proves wrong, capture what changed in the team's view and what will be done differently. This matters especially when a concern did not turn into an incident. If the only celebrated intervention is the dramatic rescue, people learn to wait for drama. Recognise the early question that prevented it.

7. **Review your own modelling.** After a difficult discussion, ask whether your response made the next concern easier or harder to raise. This is not a request for a personality assessment. Look at observable behaviour: did you invite a view, name your uncertainty, ask for evidence, and keep the issue separate from the person? Repetition is what makes safety credible.

### The exercise

Run a 55-minute CES launch session, then use the two-week sampling task in live work. Prepare five anonymised major claims from recent planning, review or decision meetings. They should be genuine types of claim the team makes, but do not use the session to expose an individual. Bring a simple page for each claim with space for the four CES conditions.

Spend 10 minutes setting the purpose: the team is learning to test claims, not grading confidence. In the next 15 minutes, take two claims and have small groups decide whether each is an assumption, a hypothesis, an evidence statement, or a test plan. Capture their reasoning. Keep the claims short enough to assess in the room. Do not debate whether the underlying proposal is attractive; identify what evidence or test would allow the team to treat it with greater confidence. Spend 15 minutes scoring the same claims against the CES: evidence available, assumption recorded, experiment defined if needed, and learning captured. Where a condition is absent, record the next action rather than an excuse.

Use the final 15 minutes to agree how the team will sample five major claims over the next two weeks and who will collect the record. At the end of the two weeks, score each sampled claim against all four conditions and publish the percentage meeting every condition. Capture the percentage, the claims sampled, the missing conditions, and one change to the team's next planning or review conversation. Improvement, not perfection, is the purpose.

### What to measure

The CES produces a percentage: the share of five sampled major claims that meet all four conditions — evidence available, assumption recorded, experiment defined if needed, and learning captured. The artefact is the two-week claim record and its published percentage, not a score for individual people.

Read it after each two-week sample in a planning, review or decision forum. Look first for the condition most often absent. A low evidence rate may point to rushed assumptions; missing learning may show that experiments are being run without changing the next decision. Read a small sample of the claims as well as the percentage, so the team can see the kind of statement that is escaping the standard. The measure is useful when it changes how the next claim is handled.

### One thing to do this week

At your next delivery review, choose one major claim that is being treated as fact. Ask whether it is evidence, an assumption, a hypothesis, or a test plan. Record the answer and the next check. Thank the person who supplies the uncertainty, even if the review now needs a short pause. Then watch your own response when somebody challenges it.

## Chapter 9 — Expectations: the clarity code

A programme has agreed a change to the order of a migration. The decision is made in a video call late on Tuesday. One engineer posts a short note in chat before signing off. A project manager assumes the revised order will appear in the delivery plan. The supplier sees neither item until Thursday, when their team begins work on the original sequence. By Friday, three people have different versions of what was decided, and each can point to a reasonable place where they expected the decision to be recorded.

Nothing in this scene requires bad intent. The chat note was visible to some people. The call was attended by the people who spoke. The plan had always been the place where schedules lived. The failure sits in the gap between those conventions. If a team has not agreed what makes a decision real, where it belongs, and who must see it, everyone supplies their own rule. The result looks like poor communication after the fact, but it began as an unspoken expectation.

### The principle

Most communication failures in delivery are failures of agreement before they are failures of effort. One person thinks a quick chat message is enough to settle a decision; another expects a formal record. One person treats direct language as the fastest way to remove ambiguity; another experiences it as aggression. One person fills every pause; another waits for an invitation that does not come. Neither a new channel nor a longer meeting fixes rules that have never been made explicit.

The **Clarity Code** is the team's agreed account of what good communication looks like in its work. Its five elements, the **5 C's**, are **Clear expectations**, **Courage**, **Clogging prevention**, **Choose the right medium**, and **Concise and clear communication**. The title matters less than the discipline. A team needs to say how decisions are recorded, what must be raised, when people may challenge, how airtime is balanced, and which channel carries which kind of information.

Clear expectations remove routine guesswork. They define, for example, the difference between a discussion and a decision, what needs an owner, and when an update is complete. Courage permits people to be open and direct. It must be joined to kindness: directness is not a licence to make colleagues smaller. Clogging prevention addresses the fact that expertise and confidence are not the same thing. When the same voices occupy every discussion, information from people with less status or a different style arrives late or not at all.

Choosing the right medium protects the team from two common errors. The first is burying a significant decision in a quick channel that is useful for coordination but poor for retrieval. The second is turning every small question into a formal process, so the record becomes too slow to use. Concise and clear communication gives a reader the context, decision, action, owner and deadline without making them reconstruct the work from a chain of messages.

The Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) collective-intelligence studies found that a more equal distribution of conversational turn-taking was associated with collective intelligence. This is not an instruction to make every contribution the same length. A security specialist may need more time on a material risk than a project observer. It is a warning against treating the loudest or most fluent contribution as the whole of the team's knowledge. The Clarity Code turns that warning into a rule the team can see and revise. It gives the manager a basis for changing the process, rather than making a private judgement about confidence.

### What it looks like when it is missing

On Thursday, the supplier asks why the migration order has changed. The project manager replies that it was agreed on Tuesday. The engineer says it is in chat. The supplier says chat is not a decision record. All three are correct according to their own working practice.

The team now spends an hour reconstructing the call, searching messages and asking who was present. The delivery plan is amended, but there is no agreement about the next change. A senior person says that people should “communicate better”. That instruction adds nothing. It does not say what belongs in the plan, when a chat item becomes a decision, or who confirms that the people affected have seen it.

In the next call, the usual two voices fill the time explaining the correction. A delivery analyst who spotted an impact on a dependent release has no defined route into the decision. The impact remains in a notebook. The team has recorded activity, but it has not created shared understanding. The same uncertainty will surface again as an apparent delivery surprise. People spend time asking who should have known, although the team never agreed how a dependency moved from an observation into a decision record.

### What it looks like when it works

Before the next migration change is discussed, the team opens its Clarity Code. It says that decisions above its agreed impact threshold are posted in a shared decision log within 24 hours. The entry uses one page: context, options, decision, evidence, owner, implications and review date. Chat remains the place for quick coordination; the log is the source of truth for the decision.

The change is therefore recorded before work begins. The supplier can find it without asking who attended the call. The delivery plan points to the decision rather than attempting to duplicate its reasoning. The analyst adds the dependent-release impact in the implications section. The decision owner checks that the affected teams have seen it.

The discussion is still direct. Someone can say that the proposed order creates risk. The Code makes that challenge expected, and the facilitator watches whether a few people are clogging the conversation. The objective is not a quieter call. It is a record and a conversation in which relevant information can arrive before the work moves. When the decision is revisited, the team can see its evidence and review date without reconstructing a memory. That makes change visible and preserves time for the next issue.

### How to do it

1. **Start with the communication failures that cost the team time.** Bring examples such as a decision that had to be rediscovered, an action with no visible owner, a risk placed in the wrong channel, or a discussion dominated by the same people. Describe the event without making it a case against an individual. You are looking for an absent rule, not a culprit.

2. **Write Clear expectations as observable agreements.** State what makes a decision complete, where it is recorded, how quickly it is recorded, who owns the follow-through, and how an action carries its deadline. Be specific about the artefacts already used by the team. “Keep people informed” is not an expectation that can be checked. “Post decisions above the agreed impact threshold in the shared decision log within 24 hours” can be checked.

3. **Define Courage with kindness.** Agree that people may challenge an idea, ask for context, and state a concern early. Agree also that the challenge addresses the work, evidence or implication rather than a person's competence or motive. Courage is useful when it makes a difficult fact available; it is not a preference for blunt language. A manager must show this distinction when a challenge is inconvenient.

4. **Address Clogging prevention in the live conversation.** Notice who is doing the talking and who holds relevant knowledge. Build a pause into significant decisions so people can add an overlooked dependency, risk or customer effect. Invite a view by role or by the work closest to it, rather than inviting a vague “any other business” response after the conclusion has formed. Do not force every person to speak on every item. Make it possible for the right person to contribute before the decision closes.

5. **Choose the right medium deliberately.** Use quick channels for coordination that loses value once the immediate work is done. Use a shared record for decisions, commitments and material implications that people will need to retrieve. Use a conversation when the issue needs exploration, disagreement or shared interpretation. Tell the team where the final record will be before the discussion begins. The medium should match the cost of missing the information, not the sender's habit.

6. **Make communication concise and clear.** For a decision or update, give the context first, then the decision or current position, the action, owner and deadline. Add the evidence and implications where they matter. This is not a demand for short messages at all costs. A concise record may need enough detail for someone absent from the call to understand why the work changed.

7. **Review the Code when work changes.** The team should not treat its first draft as permanent. A new supplier, hybrid working pattern, dependency or delivery pressure may expose a rule that no longer works. Revise the agreement in the open and tell people what changed. A norm that lives only in the memories of long-standing members is still an unspoken rule. Read the revised Code after its first use and correct unclear wording promptly.

### The exercise

Set aside 60 minutes to create the first Clarity Code. Prepare three recent communication failures: one about a decision, one about a channel, and one about access to the conversation. Also prepare the existing decision record, delivery plan, chat channel and any routine update the team already uses. The aim is to improve the team's actual working agreements, not to publish a generic list of good intentions.

Use 10 minutes to describe the three examples and identify the unspoken rule in each. Spend 25 minutes working through the 5 C's. For Clear expectations, capture the team's rules for decisions, actions, owners and deadlines. For Courage, agree how direct challenge and kindness sit together. For Clogging prevention, agree how relevant voices enter a significant discussion. For Choose the right medium, map quick coordination, decisions and issues to the existing channels. For Concise and clear communication, agree the minimum information a decision or update must carry. Test each rule against one of the prepared failures: could somebody unfamiliar with the discussion follow the rule and avoid the same loss of information?

Use 15 minutes to turn the agreements into a short, visible Clarity Code and to nominate its owner. Spend the final 10 minutes agreeing when it will be tested. At the end of the next two work cycles, run the Clarity Code Pulse. Capture the Code itself, the average rating, the spread of responses and one rule to retain or change. Name the date and forum for that review before the session ends.

### What to measure

The **Clarity Code Pulse** is a fortnightly, five-item team check. Each team member rates agreement from 1, strongly disagree, to 5, strongly agree. It measures whether the agreed Code is experienced in the work, rather than whether a document exists.

Read the average and the spread of responses at the end of the next two work cycles. Do not read the average alone. A high average with one very low score may mean that a quieter group is having a different experience of access, courage or clarity. Keep the responses visible long enough to compare the second cycle with the first, then discuss the reason for any difference. Use the response pattern to choose one rule to examine with the team.

### One thing to do this week

Choose one decision your team will make this week. Before the discussion, state where the decision will be recorded, who will own it, and when affected people will see it. Afterward, check whether the record contains the context, decision, action, owner and deadline. Send the record to one affected colleague who was not in the discussion and ask whether it can be understood without a follow-up call.

## Chapter 10 — Listening, and the habit of team building

A product manager arrives at a security review with a launch date already committed to a partner. The security lead has spent the morning tracing a weakness in a third-party component. Both have material information. Both are tired of being asked to solve the other's problem. The product manager explains the commercial consequence of delay. The security lead makes notes, looks back at the risk assessment, and waits until the explanation is complete.

The next minute determines whether the session becomes a trade between two fixed positions or a piece of joint work. The security lead can begin with a defence of controls. That response may be technically sound and still leave the product manager unheard. Or the lead can show that the deadline has been understood, ask what is fixed and what is flexible, and then explain the risk. The difference is not softness. It determines whether the team can see a smaller, staged launch that protects both concerns.

### The principle

Listening is often mistaken for waiting quietly while another person finishes. In delivery, that version of listening is expensive. People prepare their counterargument, collect the facts that support their own position, and leave a conversation believing they have communicated. The team has exchanged statements but has not tested whether the underlying concern, constraint or question was understood. Important distinctions disappear: a fixed contractual date becomes a preferred date; a control requirement becomes a preference; a request for help becomes a request for approval.

The **E.A.R.S. active-listening framework** makes listening observable. **Engage fully** means being present rather than formulating a counterargument while someone is talking. **Ask** means using clarifying questions to test understanding. **Respect** means acknowledging another perspective even when you disagree with it. **Show empathy** means recognising the experience, concern or pressure behind the words. These are not scripted phrases. They are behaviours that let a team use more of the information it already has.

The Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) collective-intelligence study found that average social sensitivity was associated with groups' collective intelligence. Social sensitivity can sound like a personal trait that a manager cannot influence. E.A.R.S. gives it a practical form. You can observe whether someone is engaged, whether their questions test understanding, whether disagreement still acknowledges another view, and whether the pressure behind a concern has been recognised. That makes listening something a team can practise rather than praise in the abstract.

Regular team building supports the same work. It is not an optional social extra placed at the edge of delivery. Personal connection gives people context for interpreting a terse message, asking for help early, and giving a colleague the benefit of the doubt when pressure rises. It also creates practical knowledge: who has experience of a system, who thinks best with a written brief, who needs time to examine a risk, and who can explain a customer consequence. In hybrid teams, those connections must be designed. They will not reliably appear between booked calls.

Team building does not mean compulsory intimacy or an event disconnected from the work. Its purpose is trust and useful knowledge of one another's strengths. A regular, modest ritual is more credible than a rare away day that leaves the working pattern untouched. The test is simple: after the team has spent time together, are people more able to seek context, ask for help and interpret a colleague's intent accurately when the delivery timetable tightens?

### What it looks like when it is missing

The product manager finishes the launch explanation. The security lead responds with a list of controls and the phrase “we cannot accept that risk”. The product manager hears refusal. The security lead hears a demand to ignore a material weakness. Neither checks what the other has actually said.

The conversation turns into prepared speeches. A programme lead tries to settle it by asking which date is more important. The team chooses a position without distinguishing the partner commitment from the first-mover concern, or the security control from the specific exposure. The matter returns later with more frustration and less time.

Outside the review, people know little about how their colleagues work. A short message from the security lead is read as obstruction. A late request from product is read as carelessness. No one asks for the context because past exchanges have taught them that the answer will be defensive. A team can still be courteous in this state. Courtesy alone does not create understanding. The issue then moves into delivery planning as two competing stories. Neither story contains enough of the other person's information to support a useful choice, so the team carries the disagreement forward instead of resolving it.

### What it looks like when it works

The security lead begins by summarising: the product manager is worried that a two-week delay will miss the partner launch and lose first-mover advantage. The product manager confirms the summary. The lead then asks which part of the date is contractual, which is commercial, and whether a staged launch is possible.

When the risk is explained, the product manager does not have to agree with every control to acknowledge the concern. The conversation has established that both people are trying to protect a customer outcome. Together, they examine a smaller launch that avoids the weakness while preserving the most time-sensitive part of the partner commitment.

The team has practised this kind of exchange in routine work. People know who can explain the component, who can test the customer effect, and who needs a written question before a difficult conversation. Their team-building time has supplied useful context, not a slogan about trust. Under pressure, they can seek help earlier because they have already learned how colleagues approach a problem. That knowledge makes the next contact more direct and less speculative. The team does not need to assume intent from a terse message when it has a regular habit of learning how its members work.

### How to do it

1. **Engage fully before responding.** Put the delivery artefact or decision in view, but do not use it as a place to hide while someone is speaking. Remove avoidable distractions. Let the person complete their account, especially when it contains uncertainty or bad news. You may be able to predict their conclusion; that does not mean you understand the constraint behind it.

2. **Ask questions that test understanding.** Use a summary followed by a check: “You are concerned that the partner date is fixed because of the launch. Have I got that right?” Ask what has changed, what is known, what is assumed, and what the consequence would be. Avoid questions designed only to expose a weakness in another position. The purpose is to make the issue more precise before the team chooses a response.

3. **Respect the perspective without pretending to agree.** Name the valid concern you have heard. A product lead can acknowledge a material control risk. A security lead can acknowledge the commercial consequence of delay. Respect does not require either person to abandon their professional judgement. It keeps disagreement attached to the work rather than to a judgement about the other person's motives.

4. **Show empathy for the pressure in the work.** Say what you understand about the experience, concern or pressure: a customer commitment, a risk that could become visible later, a dependency that leaves little room to move. Empathy is not agreement and it is not a promise to remove the pressure. It shows that the person does not have to exaggerate the problem to get it recognised.

5. **Use a rotating E.A.R.S. observer.** At major meetings, appoint one person who is not responsible for the main decision to notice listening behaviour. The observer records two examples of effective listening and one missed opportunity. They are watching the conversation, not scoring personalities. Rotate the role so that the team develops a shared view of what good listening sounds like. Reserve a few minutes at the end for the observer's record, otherwise the observation will be postponed when the agenda runs late.

6. **Create a regular team-building rhythm.** Put a small amount of time beside the delivery work at a regular interval. Use it to learn practical information about one another's strengths, current pressures and ways of working. A new member should be included deliberately. For hybrid teams, make the access equal: do not rely on information exchanged only by people who happen to be in the same room.

7. **Connect the ritual to the next piece of work.** At the end, ask what the team has learned that will help it seek help, make a decision or manage a dependency. This keeps team building grounded. If the activity reveals that someone has relevant experience, make that knowledge available when the next issue emerges. Trust grows when the team sees a practical result from the attention it gives one another. Keep that result in the relevant work record, not solely in a social note.

### The exercise

Run a 55-minute E.A.R.S. practice session before using the observation task in live meetings. Prepare one current delivery issue with two valid perspectives, such as a customer date and a material risk. Also prepare a short explanation of the E.A.R.S. behaviours and a simple observation sheet with spaces for two effective listening examples and one missed opportunity.

Spend 10 minutes introducing the issue and defining Engage fully, Ask, Respect and Show empathy. In the next 20 minutes, pair people to take each perspective. The listener must summarise the concern, ask clarifying questions, acknowledge the perspective and name the pressure behind it before giving a response. Ask the speaker to say whether the summary is accurate before the listener continues. Use 15 minutes for observers to share what they heard: not whether they liked the answer, but where E.A.R.S. was visible and where it was absent.

Use the last 10 minutes to agree the rotating E.A.R.S. observer for the next three major meetings and to choose one regular team-building ritual. After each meeting, record two examples of effective listening and one missed opportunity. At the end of the third meeting, capture one shared listening habit to maintain and one to improve. Keep the team-building ritual regular enough that practical knowledge of strengths does not depend on chance conversations. Capture the ritual's purpose and its next date, so it does not vanish when the delivery agenda becomes crowded.

### What to measure

The **E.A.R.S Listening Observation** is the record from a rotating observer at the next three major meetings. For each meeting, the observer captures two examples of effective listening and one missed opportunity. The artefact is a short behaviour record, not an assessment of who is naturally a good listener.

Read the three records at the end of the third meeting. Identify one shared listening habit to maintain and one to improve. Use examples from the record so the agreement describes a behaviour the team can recognise. Then use the next meetings to see whether the chosen habit is present in the work. The measure is most useful when it changes the team's next conversation, rather than becoming another meeting note.

### One thing to do this week

In your next difficult delivery conversation, do not lead with your response. First summarise the other person's concern and ask them to confirm or correct you. Then name one pressure you can see in their position before explaining your own view. Write down the correction you receive. It is evidence of what you would otherwise have missed.

## Chapter 11 — Conflict and feedback

Two engineers are reviewing a component that has failed twice during testing. One wants to rebuild it before the customer launch; the other wants to patch it, protect the date, and deal with the larger change later. Both have evidence, both can point to work the other has not considered, and neither trusts the other's preferred route. A programme lead has asked for an answer by the end of the day.

The argument does not begin with raised voices. It begins with increasingly careful messages that select only the facts supporting each position. The patch is described as reckless. The rebuild is described as academic. A decision could be forced by seniority, but that would leave the unchosen risk unowned and the losing engineer less likely to raise the next concern. The useful work is to turn a contest between preferences into a shared examination of what the customer launch and future stability require.

### The principle

Healthy teams do not remove conflict. They make it useful. In complex delivery, disagreement is often evidence that people are seeing different parts of the system: customer impact, operational risk, technical debt, a dependency, cost, or the consequence of delay. A team that never disagrees may be withholding information, or it may be dealing only with work that has already been simplified for it. The risk begins when the disagreement turns personal, becomes hidden, or circles without producing a decision or a test.

**L.E.A.N conflict resolution** gives the team a route through. **Listening** means understanding the other view before replying to it. **Empathy** means recognising the underlying concern. **Adaption** means changing the approach or the response in light of what has been learned. **Nurture of difference** means treating the difference as a source of stronger decisions. The spelling of Adaption is part of the named method. Its point is practical: listening and empathy have to alter the work if they are to be more than a courteous preface to the same argument.

Feedback needs the same discipline. The **Yin Yang Feedback** model balances **Yin** — reflection, receptivity and honest feedback — with **Yang** — action, innovation and adaptation. Reflection without action becomes a retrospective in which people describe the same frustrations every month. Action without reflection becomes repetition: the team moves quickly, but it cannot tell whether it is correcting the cause or simply moving the pressure to another part of the work.

The **Expression of Value Delta (EVD)** gives this feedback loop something tangible to inspect. Instead of saying, “I worked on the database” or “The task is 80% complete,” the team states what changed in value, risk, quality or learning. A database change might reduce average query time from 900ms to 540ms and move a customer-facing page-load target from 4.1 seconds to 3.4 seconds. If work did not produce movement, the update says what was learned and what changes next. The measure directs the conversation away from activity and towards consequence.

Google's Project Aristotle describes psychological safety as confidence that teammates will not embarrass or punish one another for taking interpersonal risks. That condition allows conflict and feedback to contain the truth needed for learning. L.E.A.N, Yin Yang Feedback and the EVD do not guarantee agreement. They give a team a way to disagree, reflect and act without leaving the value of the work hidden behind a percentage complete.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The engineers defend their positions in separate messages. One repeats the test failures. The other repeats the date. The programme lead reads the exchange as a choice between delivery and quality, even though both engineers are worried about both.

A decision is made to patch because the deadline is nearest. Nobody records what failure rate would make that route unacceptable, what effort the patch actually needs, or what must happen after launch. In the next retrospective, the team says that communication was poor. The item is added to a list of lessons, but no action changes the way a similar conflict will be handled.

Status reporting makes the gap harder to see. The update says that the patch is 80% complete. It does not say whether customer risk has reduced, whether performance has changed, or what was learned from the second failure. The team has activity to report but no visible movement in value, risk, quality or learning. The unrecorded difference does not disappear. It returns as resentment or a late defect, but the team cannot see whether its decision process helped or merely ended the discussion. Feedback becomes a verdict on people rather than material for the next choice.

### What it looks like when it works

The engineers begin by stating the shared concern: protect the customer launch and avoid future instability. They then use L.E.A.N. Each listens to the other route, identifies the concern underneath it, and acknowledges what the other has seen. The rebuild advocate hears the date risk. The patch advocate hears the cost of carrying a known weakness into production.

They agree to a small experiment that measures failure rate and delivery effort for the patch route. The result provides evidence for adapting the approach rather than a reason to repeat a preference. The programme lead has a decision path and a visible risk, not a forced choice between two labels.

At the next retrospective, the team uses Yin Yang Feedback. It reflects on how the disagreement was handled, receives candid feedback about the point where evidence was missing, and agrees an action for the next similar decision. The update reports the effect of the chosen work or the learning obtained if movement has not yet occurred. The feedback has produced motion, and the action has been informed by reflection. The record gives a future reviewer a clear account of what the team knew, what it tested and what changed. It is a basis for learning, not a defence of the people involved.

### How to do it

1. **Put the shared concern on the page first.** Before debating routes, ask what each person is trying to protect. In the component dispute, both customer launch and future stability belong in the statement. Write the evidence, constraints and uncertainty beside it. State what decision is needed and when it is needed, so the discussion does not drift into a general debate about the component. This reduces the temptation to describe one view as caring about delivery and the other as caring about quality.

2. **Listen to understand the other view.** Give each person enough time to set out their evidence and reasoning. Summarise it back before testing it. Listening does not suspend judgement; it stops the team from arguing with a weaker version of the other person's position. If the summary is wrong, correct it before moving to options.

3. **Name the underlying concern with empathy.** Identify the pressure beneath the stated recommendation: a customer commitment, the effect of a further failure, future instability, delivery effort or a dependency. Acknowledge that concern explicitly. This does not make all risks equal, but it stops the team from treating a real concern as a personal preference simply because it is inconvenient.

4. **Use Adaption to change the approach.** Ask what evidence, test or smaller piece of work would make the next decision better. The answer may be a patch experiment, an effort estimate, a failure-rate check or a staged route. Give the test an owner and a point at which the result will be read. A test that has no decision attached to it merely postpones the conflict. Adaptation is not compromise for its own sake. It is a response to what the team has learned through the disagreement.

5. **Nurture difference after the decision.** Record the view that was not chosen, the condition under which it should be revisited, and the owner of the next check. The difference may contain the warning that matters later. Treating it with care means the person who raised it can return with new evidence without being seen as reopening an old argument.

6. **Run Yin and Yang as one feedback loop.** Make room for Yin: reflection, receptivity and honest feedback on what occurred. Then insist on Yang: an action, innovation or adaptation that changes the next cycle. A retrospective should finish with a visible next action, an owner and a point at which the team will review it. A delivery action should be checked against what the team learned, not merely marked complete. If the action produces no movement, report that fact and decide what changes next.

7. **Report the EVD rather than activity alone.** For each meaningful update, state the work completed and the observable movement in value, risk, quality or learning. If the work moved nothing yet, say what was learned and what changes next. This creates a direct link between feedback and delivery. It also exposes when measures need to be defined before work begins. Keep this record visible to the people making the next decision.

### The exercise

Run a 60-minute conflict-and-feedback session using one current disagreement or a recent, anonymised one. Prepare the available evidence, the two views, and a blank page for the shared concern, the next test or action, and the EVD update. The purpose is not to decide who argued better. It is to practise moving from conflict to learning and action.

Use 10 minutes for each view to state what it is trying to protect. Spend 15 minutes applying L.E.A.N: listen to the other view, name the underlying concern with empathy, identify an Adaption of the approach, and capture how the difference will be nurtured after the decision. Write the condition under which the unchosen route must be revisited. In the next 15 minutes, use Yin Yang Feedback. Reflect candidly on the discussion, then agree the action or experiment that changes the next delivery cycle.

Use the final 20 minutes to agree how team updates will use the EVD for one delivery cycle. Capture the reported work, the observable value, risk, quality or learning change, and, where there is no movement, what was learned and what changes next. At the cycle's end, calculate the percentage of updates using complete EVD fields. Review whether at least 80% of reported work can be linked to an observable value, risk, quality or learning change. Where it cannot, capture which measures must be defined before work begins. Assign someone to bring that record to the cycle review and state the learning plainly.

### What to measure

The **Expression of Value Delta (EVD)** is the record of movement caused by the work: an observable value, risk, quality or learning change, rather than activity or a percentage complete. For one delivery cycle, calculate the percentage of team updates using complete EVD fields.

Read that percentage at the cycle review and check whether the team can link at least 80% of its reported work to an observable value, risk, quality or learning change. Read a few individual updates as well as the percentage, especially those without movement. Do not use a missing link as a reason to embellish an update. Use it to identify which measures need definition before the next work begins. Use examples honestly.

### One thing to do this week

Choose one live disagreement. Ask each person to state the shared concern before they argue for a route. Then agree one piece of evidence, test or action that could alter the next decision, and make the next update describe the change it produces. At the next review, ask what the team learned from that change before deciding the following action.
