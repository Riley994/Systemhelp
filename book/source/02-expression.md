## PILLAR 2: EXPRESSION

### Have Great Communication

#### Pillar Introduction

Definition. EXPRESSION is the team's ability to exchange information, ideas, concerns, uncertainty, disagreement, and learning in a way that is safe, clear, balanced, and useful. It is not merely the volume of communication. It is the quality of understanding created by that communication. The Communicate C.L.E.A.R.L.Y Toolkit® develops seven behaviours: Create safety, Lead by example, Expectations, Active listening, Regular team building, L.E.A.N conflict resolution, and Yin Yang feedback.

Why it matters. Complex work depends on information moving quickly to the people who need it. Silence, vague language, defensive meetings, and dominant voices create delays and blind spots long before they appear as missed milestones. When people fear looking ignorant, disruptive, or wrong, the team loses the very questions, warnings, and ideas it needs to succeed.

Research summary. In Woolley and colleagues' studies, collective intelligence was associated with average social sensitivity and more equal distribution of conversational turn-taking, rather than being strongly associated with average or maximum individual intelligence.1 Google's research places psychological safety at the top of its listed team dynamics and describes it as the confidence that teammates will not embarrass or punish one another for admitting a mistake, asking a question, or offering an idea.2 These findings make EXPRESSION a central performance capability, not a soft-skill add-on.

Expected outcomes. Teams will establish clear communication norms, use evidence rather than untested opinion, demonstrate active listening, resolve conflict constructively, and report progress as movement in value rather than activity. Leaders will model curiosity, fallibility, openness, and respectful challenge.

### Chapter 2.1: Create Safety and Lead by Example

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to create a non-judgmental environment for speaking up, model vulnerability and curiosity as leaders, and replace unsupported opinion with clear evidence or a test plan.

#### Explanation

Psychological safety does not mean avoiding standards, difficult conversations, or accountability. It means that people can raise concerns, ask basic questions, admit uncertainty, challenge ideas, and report mistakes without being humiliated or punished. In a safe team, the response to bad news is not "Who caused this?" but "What are we seeing, what does it mean, and what do we need to do next?"

Leaders set the emotional temperature. When a leader becomes defensive, publicly dismisses an idea, or pretends to have all the answers, other people learn to protect themselves. When a leader says, "I may be missing something — what do you see?" or "I made the wrong call here; let us learn from it," the leader signals that honesty has permission. Safety is built in small, repeated moments, not in a single workshop.

The C.L.E.A.R.L.Y Evidence Standard (CES) supports safety because it takes pressure away from personality. A claim becomes an assumption, a hypothesis, an evidence statement, or a test plan. That lets people challenge the idea without attacking the person.

#### Practical Example

During a delivery review, a senior stakeholder says, "Customers will definitely prefer the new approval journey." A team member who normally stays quiet asks, "What evidence do we have from customers who use the current journey?" Rather than defending the statement, the leader replies, "Good question. We are assuming a preference; let us record that and run a prototype test with ten customers before we commit." The conversation becomes safer because the challenge is welcomed and turned into learning.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — interpersonal risk is a performance issue. Google describes psychological safety as confidence that teammates will not embarrass or punish one another for taking interpersonal risks, including admitting mistakes, asking questions, and offering ideas.2 Harvard Business School guidance similarly emphasises that leaders build safety by treating work as a learning problem, inviting input with humility, and normalising learning from mistakes.3

### Chapter 2.2: Expectations — The Clarity Code and the 5 C's

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to define and maintain team-specific communication norms using the five C's: Clear expectations, Courage, Clogging prevention, Choose the right medium, and Concise and clear communication.

#### Explanation

Most communication breakdowns are not caused by a lack of goodwill. They are caused by unspoken rules. One person thinks a short chat message is enough to make a decision; another expects it to be captured in a formal record. One person speaks directly because they value speed; another experiences that directness as aggressive. One person fills every silence; another waits for an invitation that never comes. The result is friction, confusion, and unequal access to influence.

The Clarity Code makes the rules explicit. Clear expectations define what "good communication" looks like in this team. Courage gives people permission to be open and direct, while kindness protects respect. Clogging prevention balances conversational airtime so expertise and confidence do not become the same thing. Choosing the right medium prevents both information overload and serious issues being buried in the wrong channel. Concise and clear communication makes context, decision, action, owner, and deadline easy to find.

#### Practical Example

A remote team has a recurring problem: decisions are discussed in video calls, then rediscovered two weeks later because nobody recorded them. The team agrees that all decisions above a defined impact threshold will be posted in a shared decision log within 24 hours, using a single-page format: context, options, decision, evidence, owner, implications, and review date. Chat remains for quick coordination; the decision log becomes the source of truth.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — equal participation supports collective intelligence. Woolley and colleagues found that a more equal distribution of conversational turn-taking was associated with collective intelligence in their group studies.1 The Clarity Code turns that finding into a team norm: important information and challenge must not depend on the most confident voice owning the room.

#### TEAM IQ Measurement: Clarity Code Pulse

The Clarity Code Pulse is a fortnightly, five-item team check. Each member rates agreement from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

#### Exercises and Team Activities

#### Reflection Questions

#### Measurement Task

Use the Clarity Code Pulse at the end of the next two work cycles. Review the average score and the spread of responses. Do not focus only on the average: a high average with one very low score may signal that a quieter group is having a different experience.

### Chapter 2.3: Active Listening (E.A.R.S) and Regular Team Building

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to use the E.A.R.S active-listening framework and create team rituals that build personal connection, trust, and practical knowledge of one another's strengths.

#### Explanation

Listening is often confused with silence. Genuine listening is active: it requires attention, questions, summarising, respect, and empathy. Without it, teams turn meetings into a sequence of prepared speeches and counterarguments. People leave believing they have communicated when, in reality, they have only spoken.

The E.A.R.S framework gives teams an observable practice. Engage fully means being present, not formulating a counterargument while someone is talking. Ask means using clarifying questions to test understanding. Respect means acknowledging another perspective even when one disagrees with it. Show empathy means recognising the experience, concern, or pressure behind the words. These behaviours increase the quality of information the team can use.

Regular team building is not an optional social extra. It creates the personal context that helps people interpret one another's style, ask for help earlier, and give the benefit of the doubt during pressure. For hybrid teams, these connections must be deliberately designed rather than left to chance.

#### Practical Example

A cross-functional team has repeated tension between security and product delivery. During a listening session, a product manager explains the commercial deadline. Instead of responding with a defence of security controls, the security lead summarises: "You are worried that a two-week delay will cause us to miss the partner launch and lose the first-mover advantage. Have I got that right?" The product manager confirms. Only then does the security lead explain the risk. The team finds a smaller staged launch that protects both concerns.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — social sensitivity matters. The MIT collective-intelligence study found that average social sensitivity was associated with groups' collective intelligence.1 E.A.R.S translates social sensitivity into behaviours that teams can observe, practise, and improve.

### Chapter 2.4: L.E.A.N Conflict Resolution and Yin Yang Feedback

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to resolve conflict constructively using L.E.A.N, run feedback loops that combine reflection with action, and report progress as value movement through the Expression of Value Delta.

#### Explanation

Healthy teams do not eliminate conflict; they make conflict useful. Disagreement is often evidence that people see different parts of a complex system. The danger begins when the disagreement becomes personal, hidden, or endlessly circular. L.E.A.N provides a route through: Listening to understand the other view, Empathy for the underlying concern, Adaption of the solution or approach, and Nurture of difference as a source of stronger decisions.

Feedback must also produce motion. The Yin Yang model balances Yin — reflection, receptivity, and honest feedback — with Yang — action, innovation, and adaptation. A retrospective without action becomes venting. Action without reflection becomes repetition. The team needs both.

The Expression of Value Delta (EVD) makes this feedback cycle tangible. Instead of reporting, "I worked on the database" or "The task is 80% complete," the team reports what changed: "We delivered the database change, which reduced average query time from 900ms to 540ms. The customer-facing page-load target has moved from 4.1 seconds to 3.4 seconds." If no movement occurred, the team reports what was learned and what changes next.

#### Practical Example

Two engineers disagree about whether to rebuild a component or patch it. Using L.E.A.N, they first identify the shared concern: both want to protect the customer launch and avoid future instability. They agree on a small experiment that measures failure rate and delivery effort for the patch route. The next retrospective reviews the evidence. Instead of arguing from preference, the team adapts from learning.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — learning requires candour and action. Harvard Business School guidance notes that psychologically safe teams can share information, be transparent, and learn from errors; it also emphasises normalising opportunities to learn from mistakes.3 L.E.A.N and Yin Yang Feedback provide a structured way to turn that candour into improvement.

#### Exercises and Team Activities

#### Reflection Questions
