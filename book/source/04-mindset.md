## PILLAR 4: MINDSET

### Being Not Doing

#### Pillar Introduction

Definition. MINDSET is the team’s shared way of interpreting challenge, uncertainty, failure, difference, and opportunity. It is the belief that capability is developed through learning and that complex work is improved through intelligent experimentation rather than rigid certainty. M.I.N.D.S.E.T Mastery® includes: Make and mature a growth mindset, Innovation and experimentation, Networking and cross-functional collaboration, Distributed shared project ownership, Synergy, Education and learning, and Team Tributes.

Why it matters. Under pressure, teams often revert to defensive habits: protect the plan, hide the problem, avoid the difficult experiment, and work inside familiar silos. That response may create temporary comfort, but it reduces the team’s ability to adapt. A high-TEAM-IQ mindset uses difficulty as information. It asks, "What can we learn cheaply and early?" rather than, "How can we look certain until the deadline?"

Research summary. Harvard guidance on psychological safety describes learning behaviours such as asking for help, admitting uncertainty, and discussing mistakes as essential but potentially embarrassing interpersonal risks.3 MIT research also shows that collective intelligence is associated with social sensitivity and balanced participation, making cross-functional learning and respect for difference performance practices rather than optional culture initiatives.1

Expected outcomes. Teams will frame initiatives as testable hypotheses, expose their biggest assumptions early, learn across functional boundaries, share ownership of the outcome, and build rituals that recognise learning and individual strengths.

### Chapter 4.1: Make a Growth Mindset and Innovate

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to frame difficult work as a learning opportunity, use experimentation rather than certainty to reduce risk, and apply M.I.N.D.S.E.T Value Engineering to test assumptions quickly.

#### Explanation

A growth mindset is not positive thinking. It is the practical belief that ability, knowledge, and solutions can develop through effort, feedback, and learning. For teams, this means a failed experiment is not the same as a failed team. If a team learns early that an approach will not work, it has saved future cost, customer harm, and wasted commitment.

Innovation requires a safe place to propose ideas, test them, and learn from both success and failure. The team must be clear about the difference between a controlled experiment and careless delivery. A good experiment has a hypothesis, a smallest viable test, a measurable result, and a decision rule. That is the role of M.I.N.D.S.E.T Value Engineering (MVE).

MVE prevents teams from building elaborate solutions to untested beliefs. It asks teams to say: "If we do this action, this metric will change by this amount within this timeframe." If the evidence disagrees, the team adapts. The point is not to be right on the first attempt; it is to learn faster than the risk grows.

#### Practical Example

A product team believes that adding a personalised dashboard will increase weekly engagement. Rather than building the full feature, it prototypes a simple version for 5% of eligible users. The MVE hypothesis is: "If returning users see a personalised dashboard, weekly active usage will increase from 38% to 43% within four weeks." The result increases only to 39%. The team interviews users, learns that reminders matter more than dashboard content, and redirects effort. The first assumption did not win, but the team did.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — frame work as learning. Harvard Business School guidance on psychological safety recommends framing work as a learning problem, acknowledging fallibility, and modelling curiosity.3 MVE gives teams a disciplined way to make those behaviours part of normal delivery.

#### TEAM IQ Measurement: M.I.N.D.S.E.T Value Engineering (MVE)

MVE hypothesis: If we do [action], then [measure] will change from [baseline] to [target] within [timeframe], because [reason].

### Chapter 4.2: Networking and Distributed Shared Ownership

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to build cross-functional collaboration, create shared ownership of project outcomes, and use Risk-First Learning Loops to tackle the most consequential uncertainties early.

#### Explanation

Complex work is rarely completed by one specialism. It crosses product, technology, data, operations, security, legal, customer service, and more. When these groups only meet at formal handoffs, the team discovers dependencies late, sees only part of the system, and creates expensive rework. Networking and cross-functional collaboration create the shared context needed to make better decisions earlier.

Distributed shared ownership does not mean that everyone must do everything. It means everyone understands that the project outcome belongs to the team, not to a single function. A developer can flag a customer-experience risk; a product manager can raise a security dependency; an architect can help a delivery team make an early decision. This is what turns a collection of specialists into an intelligent system.

Risk-First Learning Loops (RLL) give the team a practical starting point. The team reviews its TRB, identifies the high-impact, high-probability risks, and deliberately tackles them before polishing low-risk work. It fails cheaply when it must fail and learns early when it can learn.

#### Practical Example

A new digital service depends on third-party data that may not be available at the required speed. The initial plan places integration near the end because the customer interface feels more visible. The cross-functional team uses RLL and moves the data test into the first iteration. It discovers the provider's response-time limitation immediately, redesigns the service around cached data, and avoids a late-stage launch failure.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — teams are interdependent by definition. Google's definition of a team highlights interdependence: teams plan work, solve problems, make decisions, and review progress in service of a specific project, and members need one another to get work done.2 Shared ownership and early cross-functional learning make this interdependence productive.

### Chapter 4.3: Synergy, Education, and Team Tributes

#### Learning Objective

Participants will be able to build team synergy through trust, interdependence, continuous learning, and visible recognition of individual strengths.

#### Explanation

Synergy is often used as a vague word for people working well together. In TEAM IQ, it has a more precise meaning: the team’s collective capability becomes greater because members understand, trust, and use one another's distinct strengths. Synergy grows when people recognise that the system is interconnected. A careless action that damages trust in one area can spread quickly through the whole team; a generous act of support can do the same in the other direction.

Education and learning opportunities are therefore not separate from delivery. Lunch-and-learn sessions, peer demos, communities of practice, cross-training, and reflection on failures all improve the team’s ability to respond to future complexity. Team Tributes make strengths visible. They are not empty applause. They name a specific capability and show how it advanced the shared outcome.

#### Practical Example

After a difficult release, a team runs a Synergy Showcase. The test engineer explains how the data analyst's early insight exposed an edge case; the data analyst recognises the support engineer who supplied customer evidence; the support engineer recognises the developer who created a diagnostic tool. The team sees that the successful outcome was not an individual hero story. It was an interdependent chain of contributions.

#### Research Callout

Research callout — unique skills need to be valued and used. The psychological-safety items referenced in Google's guidance include whether a person's unique skills and talents are valued and utilised by the team.2 Team Tributes make this question visible and help teams avoid leaving capability unused.
