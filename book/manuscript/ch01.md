## Chapter 1 — The finding nobody acts on

At the start of the delivery meeting, the room looks strong. The product lead knows the customer case. The architect can explain the technical constraints. The delivery manager has the plan, risks and dependencies. The operations lead knows where the handover will hurt. Each person has a sensible update and has done the work expected of them.

Then the discussion moves to a decision. A dependency has changed, a supplier date has slipped, and the release still has a public commitment. The product lead protects scope. The architect protects the design. Operations asks for a longer proving period. The delivery manager asks for a revised date. Nobody is wrong. Nobody can show how the choices fit together.

The meeting ends with actions, each assigned to a capable person. The status report will be refreshed. A decision will be taken elsewhere. By the next meeting, the programme has moved, but not in a direction that anyone chose together. This is not a failure of effort or talent. It is good people producing a result that falls short of their combined judgement.

### The principle

**Collective intelligence** is a measurable property of a team: its ability to perform across work that requires people to share information, reason together, decide and adapt. It is not a compliment for a friendly team, nor a claim that every member thinks alike. It concerns what the group can reliably produce when the work needs more than one perspective.

The MIT collective intelligence studies found a group-level capability not accounted for simply by the intelligence of individual members. Google's Project Aristotle reached a related managerial conclusion: what happens between team members matters to effectiveness; selecting impressive individuals is not a sufficient design for a dependable team.

That finding is routinely acknowledged, then ignored in the machinery of an organisation. Recruitment inspects qualifications, experience and individual performance. Appraisal rewards the person who rescues difficult work. Promotion panels look for personal authority, subject knowledge and visible results. These are reasonable things to inspect. They are easier to inspect than the quality of a team conversation, the clarity of a handover, or the way a group changes course when its evidence changes.

The mistake comes in the inference. A leader sees capable people and assumes the team will make capable decisions. A programme is staffed with specialists and assumed to be safe. Work is divided, names are placed beside tasks, and the result is treated as the sum of the people. It does not follow. A group can contain expertise and still fail to bring it into the decision. It can be busy and leave a consequential assumption untested. It can have an owner for every task and nobody accountable for the outcome across those tasks.

The cost is disguised as ordinary delivery friction: late discovery, rework, slow escalation, decisions revisited after work starts, and senior people drawn back into matters the team ought to resolve. A weekly status report may show activity. It cannot tell you whether people share a target, have heard the same evidence, or will act together when a constraint shifts.

The six pillars in this book are **Target**, **Expression**, **Accountability**, **Mindset**, **Insight** and **Quality**. They make a team's intended result, communication, ownership, learning, information and standard of value visible. The rest of the book takes each pillar in turn, turns it into habits and artefacts, and uses the TEAM IQ Measurement Cycle to choose where to improve next.

The six-pillar model and its practical measures are ours, not a published result from MIT or Google. Our claim is modest: inspect and improve the conditions through which a team sets a target, exchanges evidence, owns outcomes, learns, makes decisions and judges quality. You then have a disciplined way to raise collective capability and see whether it is happening.

### What it looks like when it is missing

Return to the delivery meeting. The product lead has a customer commitment in a slide deck, but the team has not seen the evidence behind it. The architect has logged a design concern in a document read only by the technical group. Operations has a service risk in a separate register. The delivery manager has a plan whose dates assume all three views can be satisfied.

When the supplier date changes, people speak from their own artefact. The discussion becomes a negotiation between local commitments. The architect protects a design decision because its effect on the customer target is unclear. The product lead protects scope because the service risk is not visible in prioritisation. Operations is asked for an answer after the decision has hardened.

The actions are individually tidy. Someone will ask the supplier for a revised date. Someone will estimate an alternative. Someone will update the plan. What is absent is a shared outcome, the evidence that should alter the decision, the person who can make the call, and the point at which the team will review the consequence. The group has distributed work without distributing understanding.

This can look orderly from outside. Meetings occur, work is assigned and papers are produced. It becomes expensive because uncertainty travels quietly between roles until it arrives as a late defect, a blocked release or a request for an executive decision.

### What it looks like when it works

In the same meeting, the changed supplier date is placed beside a visible target that names the customer or business change the release must create. The team has agreed the evidence that would justify a change in scope, date or design. The product lead explains the value at risk. The architect explains the constraint in terms the whole room can use. Operations identifies the condition required before the service can change.

The discussion is still difficult. There may be a trade-off between date, scope and assurance. The team is working on one decision rather than defending private versions of it. A concern is information that may alter the route to the shared outcome, not a challenge to someone's authority.

The meeting ends with a decision record: what has been decided, the evidence used, the open assumption, the owner of the next test and when the group will read the result. The delivery manager can change the plan because the reason is clear. The product lead can explain it to a sponsor without hiding uncertainty. Operations is involved before the handover.

This is not consensus theatre. A team can disagree sharply and still be collectively intelligent if it makes its reasoning visible, uses relevant expertise and commits to clear conditions for revisiting a course. The aim is a team that can think and act together when the original plan no longer fits the work.

### How to do it

Do not begin by rating personalities. Begin with live work that exposed a gap between individual effort and team performance: a delayed decision, failed handover, late issue or differently interpreted priority. Inspect the way the team worked, not a person to blame.

1. **Choose one outcome, not a general complaint.** Put a live delivery outcome in the centre. State the customer, business, risk or service change required; the date or decision point; and the consequence if it is not achieved. A label such as poor communication gives the group nothing observable to inspect.

2. **Collect the existing evidence before the meeting.** Bring the plan, decision record, risk entry, customer information, service data and work board. Do not produce a new pack to make the team look aligned. Gaps between the existing artefacts are evidence. If a key statement exists only in one person's head, record it as a finding.

3. **Ask for individual accounts before group discussion.** Give each person time to write, without consulting colleagues: the outcome they believe the team is pursuing; evidence they trust; the main uncertainty; the decision needed; and who owns the next move. This exposes differences before the most senior or confident voice sets the answer.

4. **Compare the accounts against the work.** Put answers beside the artefacts. Look for differences that would change a decision or fail a handover. Is the target the same in each account? Is a risk known to people who must alter the plan? Does the named owner have authority, information and support? Does a technical or operational condition appear only after a business commitment? Record the difference as a condition in the system, not a flaw in a person.

5. **Make the next decision explicit.** For the most material difference, agree the decision, evidence still needed, person obtaining it, contributors and review date. Write the assumption plainly. A decision with no stated assumption cannot be intelligently revised; it can only be defended or abandoned.

6. **Use the TEAM IQ Measurement Cycle to keep evidence connected.** The cycle is not a reporting layer added after delivery. It makes intentions, evidence, ownership, learning, decisions and outcomes visible in the work. At the end of an interval, read outcome, evidence, decision and learning together. Then decide whether the constraint concerns Target, Expression, Accountability, Mindset, Insight or Quality. Work on that before adding another ceremony.

7. **Read patterns, not isolated incidents.** One poor meeting may be a timing problem. A repeated difference between the plan's target, a risk register's evidence and a decision's assumption is a team pattern. Look across live decisions before changing a role, meeting or process. Ask whether the team can make the connection without a senior person stitching the pieces together.

8. **Keep the intervention small and observable.** Agree one change to test in the next interval: a shared target in a decision record, an operations review before commitment, or a named evidence owner. Decide what you will look for. If it does not improve the quality or timing of the decision, keep the learning and change the practice.

This procedure is our applied method, built around the course's Measurement Cycle. It is not a clinical assessment and does not turn a team into a score. It exposes whether the team can connect the information and ownership required for the work. That is a better starting point than a general request to collaborate more.

### The exercise

Run a shared-outcome review with the intact team around one live decision or delivery outcome. This is a practical book exercise, not a validated research instrument. Prepare a wall or shared board with space for target, evidence, uncertainty, decision, ownership, learning and outcome. Bring the plan, risk material, decision record and customer or operational evidence. Ask only people who affect the decision to attend.

Allow five minutes to state the outcome and decision point. Give each participant ten minutes to write their account of the target, evidence, uncertainty and expected owner. Keep this silent. Find differences before discussion smooths them away.

Spend fifteen minutes placing accounts and artefacts on the board. Group similar statements, but do not force agreement. Mark differences that could change the route, timing, scope, risk or standard of the outcome. Use fifteen minutes to select the most material difference. Agree the evidence to settle or narrow it, who will obtain it, contributors, and when the team will return to the decision.

Use the final ten minutes to capture a one-page review record: shared outcome, unresolved assumption, next evidence action, named ownership, review point and the likely constraining pillar. Read it aloud. If anyone cannot explain the outcome or their next move, correct it before leaving.

Save a photograph or export of the board with the record for the next review.

The session takes fifty-five minutes. Its output is not a verdict on team quality. It shows where understanding breaks and tests whether a clearer connection improves the next decision.

### What to measure

The first artefact is a one-page review record for a live outcome, used in the TEAM IQ Measurement Cycle. It links the intended result with available evidence, open uncertainty, decision, owner, learning and observed outcome. Keep the original record. The comparison between what the team assumed and what happened makes learning visible.

Read it at the next delivery review and whenever the decision changes. Look for recurring breaks: differently explained targets, evidence after commitment, ownership without authority, or learning that never changes a decision. These signal a pillar for attention, not individual worth.

This is a TEAM IQ design choice, not a published universal score. At this stage, measure whether the team can show its reasoning, test it and alter work when evidence requires it.

### One thing to do this week

Take a delayed or revisited decision. Ask each person involved to write the outcome, evidence they trust and uncertainty that matters. Compare the answers before anyone explains them. Do not repair every difference. Choose the one most likely to alter the decision, name the evidence needed and set the next review point. You will have a clearer diagnosis than a status update.
