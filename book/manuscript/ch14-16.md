## Chapter 14 — Being, not doing

At half past four on a Thursday, the delivery lead is looking at a release plan that has no room left in it. A new customer dashboard has been marked amber for weeks. The design is approved, the backlog is detailed, and the status report says the team is on track. In the meeting, a developer says the assumed data is not yet available in the form the dashboard needs. The product manager replies that the launch date has already been communicated. The architect suggests an interface change, but nobody writes it down because it would reopen the design.

The room moves on to actions. The dashboard remains amber. A week later, the data issue becomes a build issue, then a test issue, then a date issue. By then the team has committed too much work to ask the basic question that was available on Thursday: will the thing we plan to build produce the change we expect? This is not a failure of effort. It is a team treating certainty as safer than learning.

### The principle

**MINDSET** is a team’s shared way of interpreting challenge, uncertainty, failure, difference and opportunity. In delivery, it shows up before anyone calls it culture. It is there in the reaction to a defect that nobody expected, an estimate that no longer holds, a customer finding that contradicts a requirement, or a junior colleague who spots a weakness in a familiar design.

A growth mindset is not positive thinking and it is not a licence to congratulate people for poor work. It is the practical belief that ability, knowledge and workable approaches can develop through effort, feedback and learning. A team with this habit can say that an assumption was wrong without turning that statement into a verdict on the person who made it. It can distinguish between an experiment that did not support its premise and a delivery failure caused by unmanaged work.

That distinction matters because complex work contains assumptions whether you record them or not. A plan can look complete while its most consequential belief has never met customer evidence, operational data or a technical test. When the belief proves false late, people often call it an unforeseen issue. In many cases it was seen early but not given a safe, disciplined route to be tested.

The delivery implication is practical. If people expect blame, they will present a confident answer before they have evidence. If they expect curiosity and clear standards, they can expose the uncertain part while it is still cheap to change. A team can be demanding about preparation and still make it safe to say that it does not yet know.

Innovation needs the same discipline. New ideas should not be accepted because their sponsor is persuasive, nor rejected because the team is busy. They need a small test, a measure and a decision rule. **M.I.N.D.S.E.T Value Engineering (MVE)** gives a team a way to make that normal work. It does not make uncertainty disappear. It makes uncertainty visible early enough to act on.

This is a practice rather than a poster on a wall. A stated value about learning changes nothing if the weekly meeting rewards only assurance, if a failed test is hidden in a work item, or if people must secure permission from several layers before trying a small check. The cost is not merely slower invention. It is late rework, false confidence and people learning to protect themselves instead of helping the team see clearly.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The dashboard team has a detailed delivery plan but no recorded premise about the data. The analyst knows the source is incomplete. The developer has found a workaround that might affect response time. The product manager is trying to protect a date. Each person has useful information, but it remains attached to their role rather than becoming a team decision.

The status meeting asks whether work is green, amber or red. It does not ask what has been tested or what would change the plan. New suggestions arrive as extra scope, so they are deferred until after release. A test that produces an unwelcome result is described as a problem to fix rather than evidence to examine. The team builds more of the dashboard because visible progress feels safer than checking the premise.

When the issue finally reaches governance, the discussion starts with accountability for the delay. That is necessary, but it comes before learning. People become careful with language. The next risk is then less likely to be raised. The plan gets longer and the team’s understanding gets thinner.

### What it looks like when it works

In the same meeting, the delivery lead pauses the plan when the analyst raises the data concern. The team separates what it knows from what it assumes. It agrees that the question is not whether the full dashboard is attractive, but whether the available data can support a useful customer view within the intended service conditions.

The developer and analyst propose a narrow technical and customer test. The product manager agrees what result would justify continuing and what result would require a change of direction. The architect is present early enough to see the consequence of either outcome. Nobody is asked to guarantee success. Each person is asked to help produce evidence.

The first test may support the idea, qualify it or show that it should stop. All are useful outcomes if they occur before a large build. The team records the decision and updates the work rather than preserving the original plan for appearances. It has not become casual about delivery. It has become more precise about what it is trying to learn, and why.

### How to do it

1. Start with the initiative that carries real uncertainty, not a harmless improvement that is certain to succeed. Ask what must be true for the intended customer or operational result to happen. Keep the answer specific. A premise about data availability, customer behaviour, technical performance or an external dependency is more useful than a general wish to improve the service.

2. Put the premise where the whole delivery group can see it. Separate evidence already available from inference. This prevents a confident statement in a steering pack becoming accepted fact merely because it has been repeated. It also makes it easier for a tester, operations colleague or subject specialist to add a missing constraint without having to challenge the status of a senior person.

3. Write the MVE hypothesis in its full form: MVE hypothesis: If we do [action], then [measure] will change from [baseline] to [target] within [timeframe], because [reason]. Do not leave any bracket vague. The action must be something the team can carry out. The measure must be observable. The baseline and target make the expected movement clear. The timeframe stops a test from becoming an indefinite piece of work. The reason states the causal belief that is being examined.

4. Design the smallest viable test. That might be a prototype, a limited service path, a technical spike, a customer conversation structured around a concrete choice, or a controlled use of existing data. The test must be small in commitment, not careless in design. Decide who needs to be involved, what must be protected, what evidence will be collected and when the team will review it. A small test still needs appropriate security, operational and customer safeguards.

5. Agree the decision rule before the result is known. State what evidence would support continuing, what would lead to an adaptation and what would mean stopping. This is the discipline that separates learning from retrospective rationalisation. Without it, teams can spend weeks explaining why any result still supports the original idea.

6. Run the test and classify the hypothesis as supported, partly supported, disproved or inconclusive. These are not grades for individuals. They are a shared description of what the evidence permits the team to say. An inconclusive result may require a better measure or a different test. A disproved result may be the most valuable outcome if it prevents a larger commitment.

7. Record the decision taken because of the evidence and the time or cost avoided through early learning. Do not invent savings to make the record look useful. If the avoidance cannot yet be estimated, state the decision and the work that did not proceed. The value of the record is that a sponsor can trace the change from premise to evidence to delivery choice.

8. Change the meeting habit around the work. In planning, ask which premise has the largest consequence if it is wrong. In delivery reviews, ask what the latest evidence changed. In retrospectives, ask whether the team made it safe to challenge an assumption early. A leader sets the tone by treating a revised view as useful information, while still holding the team to clear preparation and follow-through.

MVE is particularly useful when a team has already written a large backlog. It gives people permission to reduce a broad commitment to the next question that matters. The work item is no longer simply to build a feature. It is to test whether a particular action will produce a stated change. That is a more demanding standard, but it is also a more honest one.

### The exercise

Run this as a fifty-five-minute session before significant build begins on an active initiative. Prepare one-page summaries of the intended outcome, the current evidence, the relevant constraints and the work that the team expects to start. Bring the people who can speak for product, delivery, technology, operations and the customer context. The purpose is not to create a long list of ideas. It is to select one assumption worth testing.

Start with five minutes to state the outcome and the delivery decision that is approaching. For the next ten minutes, ask each person to write the assumptions they believe must hold. Group similar assumptions and identify the one whose failure would most alter the plan. Keep the discussion on the current initiative, rather than turning it into a general retrospective.

Use fifteen minutes to draft the MVE hypothesis together. Check every part of the statement: action, measure, baseline, target, timeframe and reason. If the team cannot name a measure or baseline, capture that as a gap rather than forcing a precise-looking claim. The next ten minutes are for designing the smallest viable test and identifying the evidence source, owner and review point.

Spend the next ten minutes challenging the test. Ask whether it is safe, small enough, connected to the premise and capable of changing a decision. Agree the decision rule before anyone starts the work. Use the final five minutes to capture the MVE card, the test date, the people involved and the decision that will be revisited. At the end of the first test, classify the hypothesis as supported, partly supported, disproved or inconclusive, then record the decision taken because of the evidence and the time or cost avoided through early learning.

### What to measure

The artefact is an **MVE card** for the next major initiative, created before any significant build begins. It contains the MVE hypothesis, the smallest viable test, the decision rule, the evidence collected and the final classification. It should also record the delivery decision and any time or cost avoided through early learning, without manufactured precision.

Read the card at the review point agreed for the test and again in the next delivery planning session. A delivery lead should look across cards at the end of each work cycle for a simple pattern: are important assumptions being tested before large commitments, and are results changing what the team does? The measure is the quality of that link, not the number of cards completed.

### One thing to do this week

Choose one item already planned for build and write a single MVE hypothesis with the people closest to it. Do not wait for a new initiative or a formal workshop. Identify the assumption that would most damage the outcome if it proved wrong, agree the smallest viable test and put the review date into the team’s existing delivery rhythm. The useful result this week is a clear question with a route to evidence.

## Chapter 15 — Distributed ownership

The release planning board is crowded with work that appears to belong to other teams. The product manager has a customer commitment. The delivery manager has a date. A security review is marked as an external dependency, as is a data-provider response and a change to the contact-centre script. Each line has an owner, but the owners are not in the room. The group spends most of the meeting arranging handoffs and deciding who will chase whom.

Near the end, an operations lead points out that a planned service change will create a support pattern nobody has designed for. The point is acknowledged and copied into an action log. It is not connected to the customer commitment, the data dependency or the release decision. By the next planning session, the product work has moved forward and the operational concern has not. Everybody has done their own job. The outcome has no owner who can see the whole path.

### The principle

Complex delivery crosses specialisms. Product, technology, data, operations, security, legal, customer service and suppliers all hold a part of the answer. The familiar response is to make each boundary a handoff: one team completes its task, sends something on and waits for the next group to respond. That can look orderly in a plan. It often hides the important work until it is late, when the cost of changing direction is highest.

Distributed shared ownership is the team’s answer to that problem. It does not mean that everyone does everything, or that specialist accountability becomes blurred. A security professional still owns security expertise. A product manager still makes product decisions. A developer still has responsibility for sound engineering. The difference is that the project outcome belongs to the delivery group, not to the function with the most visible task.

This changes the standard for participation. A developer can raise a customer-experience risk. A product manager can bring forward a security dependency. An architect can help the delivery team make an early decision about an interface. An operations lead can challenge a release that would leave support unprepared. None needs to wait until a task is formally assigned before making a relevant risk visible.

Interdependence needs to be visible in planning, problem solving, decisions and progress reviews. That is more exacting than collaboration as a calendar invitation. If people only meet at formal handoffs, they do not have shared context when a trade-off is needed. The work may be connected on a plan, but the people making choices are not connected in time.

**Risk-First Learning Loops (RLL)** provide the working discipline. The team reviews its **Target Risk Burn-Down (TRB)**, identifies high-impact, high-probability risks and tackles them before polishing low-risk work. This does not mean ignoring important planned delivery. It means putting learning where the consequence of being wrong is greatest. A risk discovered in an early test is still work. A risk discovered during a launch is often disruption for customers and staff as well.

The cost of absent shared ownership is rarely a dramatic refusal to help. More often it is polite separation. People deliver their local output, report their local status and assume the project manager will join the dots. The project manager becomes a traffic controller, overloaded with dependencies that people closest to the work could have exposed and examined together.

### What it looks like when it is missing

The team plans the customer interface first because it is visible. The third-party data integration is placed later because another group owns the supplier relationship. The security colleague receives a request when a design is already fixed. Operations sees the service only when a release note arrives. Each function gives a reasonable answer within its own remit, but the answers do not meet until the date is close.

The TRB exists as a governance document. It is updated for reporting and read before a formal review, not used to choose work. The most serious risks remain on a list while the team finishes lower-risk interface details. A person who notices a cross-functional concern has to decide whether it is their place to raise it. Often they wait for a meeting with the nominal owner.

When the data provider cannot meet the required response time, the integration becomes urgent. The team now has to redesign around a dependency while preserving the work already built. The event is described as a late surprise, though the dependency was visible from the outset. Ownership was delegated in name, but the outcome was not held in common.

### What it looks like when it works

At planning, the team puts the customer commitment, data dependency, security requirement and support change on the same board. The product manager describes the intended customer outcome. The data specialist explains the uncertainty in the provider response. Operations explains what a change in service behaviour would mean for staff. The architect and developer identify an early path through the integration.

The group reviews the TRB together and sees that the provider response-time risk has both high impact and high probability. It moves a data test into the first iteration, before the customer interface is polished. The supplier contact is invited to the right discussion. Security and operations help define the limits of the test rather than receiving a finished design later.

The provider limitation appears quickly. The team redesigns around cached data and adjusts the work before a large build depends on the original premise. The product manager owns the customer decision, the technical people own the design choices and operations owns readiness, but all understand why the change is necessary. The group has used its connections to protect the outcome, not merely to pass work around.

### How to do it

1. Name the outcome that the group owns together. Make it concrete enough that product, technology and operations can each describe their contribution to the same result. Do not begin with a list of departmental deliverables. That list may be necessary for planning, but it does not create a shared view of success or expose the points where one local decision damages another part of the work.

2. Map the people and functions that have information, authority or work needed for that outcome. Include the less visible functions early: service operations, security, legal, data, procurement, customer support and suppliers where they affect the path. This is networking with a delivery purpose. The aim is not to enlarge every meeting. It is to make the relevant connections before an issue becomes a formal escalation.

3. Bring the connected group to the TRB. The Target Risk Burn-Down identifies assumptions, dependencies and obstacles most likely to prevent the target from being achieved. For each current risk, make the impact and probability visible and ask who has evidence that could change the assessment. A risk held by one function becomes useful to the team only when the consequence and next learning step are understood across the relevant roles.

4. Use RLL to select the work that should happen first. Identify the high-impact, high-probability risks, then design an experiment or investigation that reduces uncertainty. Resist the pull of visible but low-risk polish. A refined screen, completed document or finished component can create a reassuring progress report while the major dependency remains untested.

5. Give the learning activity an owner and give the outcome multiple active participants. One person may coordinate the test, but product may need to define the customer consequence, technical colleagues may need to establish feasibility and operations may need to set service conditions. State who will decide after the evidence arrives. This preserves clear accountability without pretending that a cross-functional answer can be produced by one role alone.

6. Make connections routine. Use a short cross-functional review in the existing work cycle to inspect the leading risks, new dependencies and changed assumptions. Ask people to bring a concern before they have a fully formed answer. The useful contribution may be a question, a data point or a constraint. If the forum only accepts finished proposals, it will recreate the handoff behaviour it is meant to replace.

7. Make ownership visible in the actions that follow. A record should show the risk, the people needed, the learning activity, the decision point and the consequence for the shared outcome. Avoid a long action list with no indication of why each item matters. When a decision changes the plan, tell all affected functions promptly so that a local team is not working to a superseded assumption.

8. Review whether the group is learning in the right order. RLL is not a demand that every activity concern risk. It is a check on priority. If high-risk learning repeatedly loses out to lower-risk feature polish, the team has a planning problem, not a motivation problem. Bring that pattern to the delivery lead and sponsor as evidence that the sequence needs to change.

Distributed shared ownership becomes credible when it is visible in decisions. It is not created by adding the word shared to a responsibility matrix. People need access to the context, an invitation to surface what they see and a method for deciding which uncertainty deserves attention. The more functions involved, the more valuable those habits become.

### The exercise

Run a fifty-five-minute RLL session at the start of an active work cycle with the people connected to the outcome. Prepare the current TRB, the planned work for the next two work cycles and any known customer, technical, operational or supplier constraints. Ask participants to arrive ready to explain a risk or dependency from their own part of the work. The session is a prioritisation discussion, not a general project update.

Use the first five minutes to restate the shared outcome and the decision the team is trying to protect. Spend ten minutes reviewing the TRB, checking that the highest risks still have a current impact and probability assessment. For the next ten minutes, identify the top third of TRB risks and ask what evidence would reduce uncertainty on each one. Keep the focus on risks that are both consequential and plausible.

Use fifteen minutes to design planned experiments for the next two work cycles. For each one, capture the risk, the relevant functions, the smallest learning activity, the evidence expected and the decision that could follow. Do not assign the risk away to one group. Make clear who needs to participate or supply information so that the learning does not become another handoff.

Reserve ten minutes to compare these experiments with lower-risk feature polish already planned. Move work where needed so high-risk learning gets priority. In the final five minutes, record the percentage of planned experiments focused on the top third of TRB risks. The target is not complete coverage in every cycle. The point is evidence that consequential learning receives priority before lower-risk feature polish.

### What to measure

The artefact is the **Risk-First Learning Loops (RLL)** record, read alongside the current TRB. For the next two work cycles, record the percentage of planned experiments focused on the top third of TRB risks. Keep the planned experiment, the risk it addresses and the evidence expected together, so the percentage does not become a reporting exercise detached from delivery.

Read the record at the start and end of each work cycle. At the end, check whether the planned experiments actually examined the risks that were prioritised and whether the results changed the TRB or work sequence. The measure is not a demand for complete coverage every time. It is evidence that high-risk learning received priority before lower-risk feature polish.

### One thing to do this week

Bring the current TRB to the next planning meeting and ask the people in the room to identify the top third of risks together. Select one high-impact, high-probability risk that has been left behind a handoff, then agree the smallest learning activity and the functions needed to run it. Put that activity ahead of one piece of low-risk polish already on the board.

## Chapter 16 — The whole is more than the parts

The service has been restored after a difficult release, but the release review is already becoming a familiar story. The delivery manager thanks the engineer who stayed late. The engineer credits the team. A slide lists completed actions: patch applied, monitoring adjusted, customer message sent. The analyst who spotted the pattern in the support data is not mentioned. Neither is the support colleague who explained the customer impact, nor the tester who had raised a related edge case earlier in the week.

The meeting closes with a request to be more careful next time. People return to their own queues. The useful knowledge is scattered across incident notes, chat messages and individual memory. A new team member could read the action list and still fail to understand how the release was recovered, which capabilities were needed, or what should be practised before the next change. The team has handled the event, but it has not yet turned the event into collective capability.

### The principle

A team can contain strong people and still leave much of its capability unused. The work may be divided sensibly, but people may not know what colleagues can contribute beyond their formal role. They may not understand how one person’s customer evidence changes a technical decision, how a tester’s pattern recognition changes an operational check, or how a support colleague’s practical knowledge can prevent a design mistake. This is where the team becomes more capable as a group, rather than merely a set of capable individuals.

**Synergy and Learning Signal** means collective capability is greater because people understand, trust and use one another’s distinct strengths. It is not a vague claim that everyone gets on. It is visible in the way information travels, in how a team responds when one part of the service changes, and in whether people can call on knowledge outside their own speciality before a problem becomes expensive.

Education and learning are therefore delivery work. Lunch-and-learn sessions, peer demos, communities of practice, cross-training and reflection on failures create routes by which useful knowledge moves. They should not be treated as optional activity for quiet periods. A team that cannot explain its service to one another, share a new constraint or learn from a difficult release will repeat avoidable work even when individuals are conscientious.

Recognition also has a practical role. **Team Tributes** make a strength visible by naming a specific capability and showing how it advanced a shared outcome. They are not general praise, social reward or a substitute for addressing poor performance. A useful tribute makes a contribution legible to the group: what someone noticed, did or enabled; what it affected; and why that contribution mattered to value, quality, risk or learning.

The question of whether a person’s unique skills and talents are valued and used is not answered by an annual recognition scheme. It is answered in the daily allocation of work, in who is invited to solve a problem and in whether the team can see contribution types that are less visible than a presentation, a senior role or a late-night recovery.

This book’s claim is a practical one rather than a guaranteed result: teams that deliberately share learning and recognise specific contributions give themselves more usable options when the next difficult decision arrives. The alternative is to depend on a small set of people and to discover missing knowledge only after it is needed.

### What it looks like when it is missing

After the difficult release, the incident record describes the technical fix. The support evidence sits in a separate system. The analyst’s work is visible only to the manager who asked for it. The tester’s earlier concern is treated as an item that was overtaken by events. Nobody connects these contributions into an account of how the team understood the problem.

Learning is postponed until there is time. A useful demonstration is cancelled because delivery is busy. A new operational constraint is explained once in a specialist meeting but never reaches the people designing the next change. The same questions recur, and newer colleagues learn by interrupting the few people who have seen the situation before.

Recognition follows visibility. The loudest role, the person who presents to leaders or the person who stays late receives thanks. Quieter work that prevented harm, produced evidence or enabled someone else’s decision is not named. People learn what is noticed, then orient their effort towards being seen rather than towards the shared outcome.

### What it looks like when it works

The review starts with the customer and service outcome, then traces the chain of contributions that changed it. The analyst shows how support data exposed the pattern. The support colleague explains the practical effect on customers. The tester describes the earlier edge case. The engineer explains the diagnostic change and the recovery. People see the linked work without reducing it to a hero story.

The team captures one lesson that must change the next release and chooses a short peer demonstration to spread it. A colleague from another function attends because the lesson affects their part of the service. The discussion makes a new connection: the support pattern can become an early release check, and the diagnostic information can be available before customers report the issue.

Team Tributes name the distinct contributions with evidence. They connect each contribution to a value, quality, risk or learning outcome. The people involved do not need to become generalists. They become easier to work with because others understand what they know and when to involve them. The next release begins with more shared context than the last one.

### How to do it

1. Treat each significant piece of delivery work as a source of learning, not only failures. Look at releases, customer findings, avoided risks, difficult decisions and changes that went well. Ask what the team now knows that it did not know before, and who else needs that knowledge. Keep the learning tied to a real service or outcome. A generic presentation about good practice will travel less well than a concrete account of a decision and its evidence.

2. Choose the learning route to fit the need. A lunch-and-learn session can introduce a topic across a wider group. A peer demo can show a concrete technique or service behaviour. A community of practice can connect people facing recurring questions. Cross-training can reduce a single point of dependence. Reflection on a failure can expose an assumption that needs to change. Do not schedule all of them. Select the one that will help the next piece of work.

3. Prepare learning material from delivery artefacts people already use: a release record, a customer finding, a risk, a design decision, a test result or an operational observation. State the context, what the team expected, what it observed, the decision it made and what it will do differently. Remove sensitive details where required, but do not sand away the practical constraint that made the learning useful.

4. Create a short, regular slot for the chosen activity and protect it from being continually displaced by routine reporting. The time does not need to be long. It needs a named purpose and an output that will be used. For a peer demo, the output may be a changed check. For cross-training, it may be another person able to carry out a task. For a reflection, it may be an updated assumption or risk.

5. Use Team Tributes at the end of a work cycle or after a material event. Name the contribution precisely. Describe the capability, the evidence of what it enabled and the link to a value, quality, risk or learning outcome. A tribute such as thanks for working hard is too vague to teach the team anything. A tribute that identifies how a colleague’s customer evidence changed a release decision tells others when that strength matters.

6. Make the recognition broad enough to reveal the real chain of work. Ask whose contribution is not normally visible in the main delivery meeting. Look across product, technical, operational, analytical, assurance and customer-facing roles. This does not mean distributing praise mechanically. It means checking whether the team’s account of success reflects the interdependent work that actually occurred.

7. Turn recognition into future capability. When a tribute identifies a useful strength, ask how the team can use it again: invite that person into an earlier discussion, pair them with another role, record their method in a peer demo, or add their insight to a release check. Recognition becomes empty if it ends with applause. It becomes useful when it changes access to knowledge.

8. Review the pattern over time. If one type of role is always recognised and another is absent, investigate the work system rather than assuming some people contribute less. It may show that evidence is not travelling, that meetings exclude key voices or that the team lacks a way to see enabling work. The aim is not an even distribution of mentions. It is a more accurate picture of the capability on which delivery depends.

Education and Team Tributes work together. Education makes knowledge available; Team Tributes show which knowledge and behaviours made a difference in real work. Both need evidence, a connection to outcomes and a place in the delivery rhythm. Used this way, they help a team retain learning after the urgent moment has passed.

### The exercise

Run this as a fifty-five-minute end-of-cycle learning and recognition session after a release, a material decision or a completed work cycle. Prepare the delivery artefacts that show the outcome: relevant risk or quality records, customer evidence, release notes, test results and any decision record. Invite people from the functions that contributed, including those whose work is usually visible only through a handoff. The purpose is to understand the contribution chain and make future learning usable.

Use the first five minutes to state the outcome and the evidence available. Spend ten minutes mapping the sequence of contributions: what was observed, who supplied information, what decision changed and what action followed. Keep the map factual. The group is not there to decide who deserves general praise.

Use the next ten minutes to identify one lesson that should alter future work. Choose the appropriate learning route: a lunch-and-learn session, peer demo, community of practice, cross-training or reflection on a failure. In fifteen minutes, record at least three evidence-based Team Tributes linked to a value, quality, risk or learning outcome. Each should name the specific capability and how it advanced the shared outcome.

Spend ten minutes deciding how the identified strengths and lesson will be used in the next work cycle. This might mean an earlier invitation to a decision, a peer demonstration or a changed delivery check. Use the final five minutes to confirm where the learning record and Team Tributes will be kept. At the end of each work cycle, review the record; quarterly, review whether recognition reaches all functions and contribution types, not only the most visible roles.

### What to measure

The artefact is a cycle-end record containing evidence-based Team Tributes and the learning action they produced. It records at least three tributes linked to a value, quality, risk or learning outcome, alongside the relevant delivery evidence. It should also show the education or learning activity selected and where the team will use the lesson.

Read it at the end of each work cycle, when the contributions and evidence are still clear. Review it quarterly to see whether recognition reaches all functions and contribution types, not only the most visible roles, and whether learning is changing future work. The measure is not the amount of praise. It is whether specific strengths are visible, valued and used.

### One thing to do this week

At the next team review, take one completed piece of work and ask the group to name three specific contributions that changed the result. Link each to a value, quality, risk or learning outcome, then choose one strength the team will involve earlier next time. Capture the decision with the existing delivery record so the recognition also becomes a practical reminder of how the work was done.
